2The story that runs through all of them

Memorise this. It is the single most important 40 seconds of any of these interviews, because it turns your unusual CV from a question mark into the reason to hire you.

Your career narrative — say it exactly like this

"I spent my first five years at Jumia Group with the local team, running end-to-end fulfillment. That's where I learned to own outcomes — I defined the KPIs and built the reporting management used to plan and to decide. Then the central team selected me, so I was working half local and half central: they brought me into central projects to improve the processes and to train teams across countries. During that work we needed an internal help centre to make the teams more productive, and the engineering team had no capacity — so I built it. When it shipped, they offered me a place with the central engineering team, delivering the internal platforms the business teams run on, and I moved in January 2024. So it wasn't a pivot away from operations — it happened because I kept being handed problems outside my scope and solving them."

Why this works: the move was offered to you on the strength of what you delivered — that's a recommendation, not a career change, and it's far stronger than any "I wanted to learn to code" story. It also quietly establishes three things without you claiming them: the central team trusted you beyond your site, you shipped something the dev team couldn't get to, and you were studying development while running a warehouse full time. Close on the last line and stop talking.

Two rules for telling it

  • Never say you were blocked, stuck, or frustrated. You weren't running from operations. Every step was something you were asked to do, or offered to do, and then got recognised for.
  • Never diminish the supply chain work — no "it was just moving boxes", no "the real value was the data". You ran the operation and you were additionally asked to improve it. Both are achievements; framing one as the boring part costs you the five years of experience you're selling.

4Numbers to fill in before you interview

These blanks appear highlighted like this throughout the files. Fill them with real figures — a rough but honest number ("roughly 800 orders a day across about 40 sellers") beats a blank every time, and interviewers ask for scale within the first five minutes.

  • Daily or monthly order volume you handled
  • Number of 3P sellers in your scope
  • Team size you led, and how many shifts
  • Return rate or failed-delivery rate — before and after
  • Defect / rejection rate caught at inbound inspection
  • Dispatch SLA % you held and the target
  • Real WMS / ERP name you worked in
  • Number of SKUs under management
  • Size of your current engineering team and sprint length

5How to use these files

The 7-day method

  • Day 1 — read. Read your role's file end to end once. Don't memorise yet, just see the shape of it.
  • Day 2 — fill the blanks. Replace every highlighted placeholder with your real number.
  • Day 3–4 — say it out loud. Not in your head. Out loud, standing up. The gap between reading fluently and speaking fluently is the whole game.
  • Day 5 — record yourself. Phone voice memo, three questions. Listen back once. You will hear filler words you don't know you say.
  • Day 6 — the transcript. Read the mock interview at the bottom of your file, out loud, playing both parts. This is where you learn to handle the follow-up, which is where most candidates lose it.
  • Day 7 — compress. Reduce each answer to three bullet points on one page. Walk in with that page, not with these files.

Three rules for the room

  • Answer in 90 seconds, then stop. The most common failure is not a bad answer, it's a good answer that ran for four minutes. Say your point, give one example, stop talking. Silence is their job to fill.
  • Never say "we" when you mean "I". You will do this constantly because you led a team and it feels polite. Interviewers cannot score "we". Say what you decided, built, or chose.
  • Bring the number, then the mechanism. "Return rate dropped" is nothing. "Return rate dropped because I root-cause-coded every return and found two sellers driving a third of the volume" is a hire.
