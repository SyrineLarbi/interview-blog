Syrine Larbi · The final file · Say it out loud

The 60-minute interview, minute by minute.

Every question they can ask — the job, your past work, and the psychology questions — with the answer written the way you should speak it. Short. Simple words. No long sentences. If an answer here takes more than 30 seconds to read out loud, it is too long and you should cut it.

112 questions 7 parts = 60 minutes Answer, then stop

How to read this file

  • Green box = the words you say. Say them out loud. Do not read them in your head.
  • Grey box = why it works. Read it once, then forget it. It is not for the room.
  • Red box = do not say this. These are the sentences that lose the job.
  • Yellow highlight = your real number goes here. Fill it before you interview. A rough honest number beats a blank.
  • Answers are short on purpose. Short is confident. Long sounds unsure.
The one rule above all others: answer the question, give one example, then stop talking. Most people do not fail on a bad answer. They fail on a good answer that lasted four minutes. Silence after your answer is not your problem to fix. Let them fill it.

0Before you walk in

Ten minutes of preparation that change the first impression. Do all of it.

The ten minutes before

  • Drink water. A dry mouth makes you sound nervous when you are not.
  • Say your first answer out loud once, in the car or the corridor. It warms up your voice.
  • One page in front of you: five numbers, five stories, three questions for them.
  • Phone off. Not silent. Off.
  • If it is a video call: camera at eye level, light in front of you, window behind the screen not behind you.
  • Decide now that you will speak slower than feels normal. Nerves speed you up by about 20%.
  • Smile in the first three seconds. People decide if they like you before you say anything useful.

Three phrases that buy you time

You will get a question you did not expect. Everyone does. Do not fill the gap with "ehh". Use one of these:

Say this

"Good question. Let me think for a second."

"Let me make sure I understood — you're asking about X, right?"

"I'll answer that in two parts."

Why it works: all three are normal, professional sentences. Silence for three seconds looks calm. "Ehh" for three seconds looks lost. Same time, different job offer.

English — you are not a native speaker, and that is fine

  • Short sentences win. Every answer in this file is short sentences. That is not a simplification for you — it is how confident people actually speak.
  • Never apologise for your English. Not once. You work in English every day.
  • If you lose a word, describe it. "The system that stores the stock" is a perfect sentence. Nobody cares that you did not say "WMS".
  • If you don't understand the question, ask. "Sorry, can you say that another way?" This is a sign of confidence, not weakness. Answering the wrong question is much worse.

1Minutes 0–5 · The opening

Small talk, then the two questions everybody asks. This part decides how the rest of the hour feels. Warm, short, confident.

1. How are you? / Did you find us okay?Warm-up
Say this

"Very well, thank you. A little excited — I've been looking forward to this one."

Why it works: "excited" is honest and it gives them energy. Do not say "fine" and stop. Do not talk about traffic for a minute either.
2. Tell me about yourself.Most important
Say this — about 45 seconds

"I'm Syrine. I spent my first five years at Jumia Group with the local team, running end-to-end fulfillment."

"That's where I learned to own outcomes. I defined the KPIs and built the reporting management used to plan and to decide."

"Then the central team selected me. So for a while I was half local, half central — they brought me into central projects to improve the processes and to train teams across countries."

"During that work we needed an internal help centre to make the teams more productive. The engineering team had no capacity, so I built it."

"When it shipped, they offered me a place with the central engineering team — the one that delivers the internal platforms the business teams run on. I moved in January 2024."

"So I've been on both sides: the team that feels the problem, and the team that builds the fix. That's why product is the natural next step for me."

Why it works: it is a story with one direction, not a list of jobs. Three things land without you claiming any of them: the central team picked you out, you worked across countries, and you shipped the thing engineering couldn't get to. The last line tells them what to do with all of it.
The two words that carry this answer: "selected me" and "no capacity." The first says someone senior judged you and chose you. The second says you filled a real gap, not a hobby project. Say both slowly.
Do not say: "I was born in..." — they mean your professional story. Do not go year by year. Do not go past 60 seconds.
3. Walk me through your CV.
Say this

"Three steps. Master's, then five years in supply chain at Jumia, then central engineering at Jumia since January 2024."

"Two things the page doesn't show. The steps overlap — I did the development diploma while I was still running the operation. And in the last part of the supply chain years I was already working half with the central team, on projects across countries."

Why it works: "the part that isn't on the page" makes them lean in. It is the same CV, but you are now the person explaining it instead of the person being checked.
4. Why are you leaving Jumia? / Why are you looking?Trap
Say this

"I'm not running from Jumia. Jumia trained me and promoted me internally. I'm going toward a job that uses all of me."

"Right now I use the engineering half. The operations half, the data half, and the part that talks to users sit unused. Product uses all three at once."

Why it works: positive, specific, and not about money or a bad manager. "Toward, not away from" is the whole trick.
Never say: my manager, the salary, the politics, I'm bored, there's no growth. Even if all four are true. One negative sentence about a current employer costs more than any answer gains.
5. Why this company? / Why us?
Say this — put their real details in

"Two reasons. First, the problem. Learning in Arabic is badly served, and I can read the users in their own language — that's not a small advantage for this product."

"Second, the stage. A team of your size still lets one person own a problem end to end. I don't want a job where I write a document and hand it away."

Why it works: one reason about them, one about you. Both are specific. Generic praise ("you're a leader in the market") tells them you applied to forty companies.
Do this the night before: use their product for 20 minutes. Write down two things you liked and one thing that confused you. That is the strongest answer in the whole interview and almost nobody does it.
6. What do you know about us?
Say this

"You build an AI learning platform for students in the region. I signed up and used it for half an hour. The practice flow was the part I liked. The part I didn't understand was X — I'd want to know if that's on purpose."

Why it works: you used it. From this moment you are a candidate who does the work, not a candidate who reads the website.
7. Why product management, when your title is engineer?Hard
Say this

"Because the part I'm best at is the part before the code. Deciding what is actually worth building."

"In the warehouse I did that every day. I found the real problem, picked what we fixed first, and proved it afterwards with the numbers. Then I learned to build. Product is those two things in one job."

Why it works: you are not asking to change career. You are saying the career already happened and the title is catching up.
8. You've never held the PM title. Why should we take the risk?The decisive one
Say this — then stop and let them answer

"That's fair, and I won't argue with it. So let me tell you what you get instead."

"You get someone who has owned a live operation with real money and real customers. Someone who built the reporting people made decisions on. And someone who can sit with engineers and understand exactly what they're telling you, because I am one."

"The PM title I don't have. The judgement underneath it I've been doing for five years."

Why it works: you agree first. Arguing with a true objection makes it bigger. Agreeing and then changing the subject to your evidence makes it smaller.
Do not say: "product management is basically what I already do" — it sounds like you think the job is easy. Respect the job, then show you can do it.

2Minutes 5–18 · Your past work — operations

Five years of supply chain. They are testing scale, ownership, and whether you actually decided things or just followed a process. Numbers first, then the mechanism.

9. What did you actually do at Jumia in supply chain?
Say this

"I was on the local team, and I owned the full chain for third-party sellers who kept their stock in our warehouse. Their stock arrived, I received it, I inspected it, I stored it. When an order came in, my team picked it, packed it, and dispatched it. If it came back, I handled the return."

"One owner, from the truck to the customer's door, and back."

"Later the central team pulled me into their projects too, so the last part of that period was half local operations, half central process work across countries."

Why it works: it is one sentence per step, in order. Anybody understands it, even someone who has never seen a warehouse.
10. What scale? How many orders, how many sellers?Fill this in
Say this

"Around 800 orders a day, across roughly 40 sellers and about X thousand SKUs. In peak season it went up by X times."

Do not say "a lot" or "it varied". An honest estimate is fine. A blank makes the whole five years sound small.
11. How big was your team?Fill this in
Say this

"X people, across X shifts. I planned the shifts against the order volume, trained them, and handled the performance conversations."

Why it works: any team size is good. What they want to hear is "I planned, trained, and had the hard conversations" — that is management, whether it is 4 people or 40.
12. What did a normal day look like?
Say this

"Morning: check yesterday's numbers — what dispatched, what didn't, what came back. Then plan the day against the order volume and the people I had."

"Middle of the day: the exceptions. A damaged delivery, a seller sending stock that doesn't match the listing, a carrier that didn't collect."

"End of the day: dispatch cut-off, and then the part that mattered most — why anything missed."

Why it works: it shows a rhythm of plan, exception, review. That is the same rhythm as a product team's week, and they will notice.
13. How did you use data in that job?
Say this

"I built the KPI set the operation ran on: dispatch SLA, picking accuracy, inventory accuracy, defect rate, return rate, failed deliveries."

"Then I built the Power BI and Excel reports on top, so management and my team looked at the same screen instead of arguing from memory."

"The important part isn't the dashboard. It's that each number had one owner and one action attached. A number nobody acts on is decoration."

Why it works: the last line is a product manager's line. It separates you from everyone who says "I made dashboards".
14. Give me an example of a problem you found in the data.Your best story
Say this

"Returns. Everyone treated them as normal cost. I started coding every single return with a reason — wrong item, damaged, listing didn't match, customer refused."

"After a few weeks it wasn't noise any more, it was a list. A small number of sellers were driving a big share of the returns, and a big share of those were listing problems, not delivery problems."

"So we fixed the listings, tightened packaging rules for those sellers, and escalated the ones that didn't improve. Return rate moved from X% to Y%."

Why it works: problem, method, finding, action, result. Five beats. This is the story that makes you a product person — you turned a feeling into a dataset and then into a decision.
Prepare the follow-up: "How did you know the coding was accurate?" Answer: "I did the first weeks myself, then wrote the rules down and audited a sample every week."
15. Tell me about a process you changed.
Say this

"Inbound inspection. We were finding damaged and wrong products late — sometimes after the customer got them. That's the most expensive place to find a problem."

"So I moved the check to the moment the stock arrived, wrote the standard down in one page, and trained the team on it. Bad stock got rejected at the door instead of travelling through the whole chain."

Why it works: "find the problem earlier, it's cheaper" is a principle, not a story. Principles are what get remembered after you leave the room.
16. What was the hardest problem you faced there?
Say this

"Failed deliveries. It's the worst kind of problem because it's not one problem. Bad address, customer not home, customer refuses at the door, carrier didn't try properly."

"I split them by cause instead of treating them as one number. Different causes, different owners — some were customer service, some were the carrier, some were the seller's listing. Once it was split, each one had somebody who could actually fix it."

Why it works: "I split one big number into causes with owners" is exactly what a product manager does with a metric that is going the wrong way.
17. Tell me about peak season. What breaks?
Say this

"Everything you were tolerating. If your process has a weak step, normal volume hides it and peak volume shows it."

"What I learned: prepare the bottleneck, not the whole system. There is always one step that decides your day. Fix that step's capacity and the rest follows."

Why it works: short, quotable, and true in software too. They will hear a person who thinks in systems.
18. Tell me about a conflict with a seller or a partner.STAR
Say this

"We had sellers whose products came back far more than everyone else's. They believed it was our delivery. We believed it was their product and their listing."

"Instead of arguing, I showed them their own return reasons next to the average. Same warehouse, same carrier, same city — different result."

"Most of them changed the listing or the packaging. The ones who didn't, we escalated. Data ended the argument — nobody argues with their own numbers for long."

Why it works: it shows you handle conflict with evidence, not authority and not avoidance. That is exactly how you will have to handle a stakeholder in this job.
19. Tell me about managing someone who wasn't performing.Management
Say this

"First I check if it's a skill problem or a will problem, because the fix is different."

"Most of the time it was skill — nobody had actually shown them the standard. So I trained, wrote it down, and checked again in a week."

"When it was will, I said it plainly and early, in private, with the specific example. People can fix what you name. Nobody can fix 'you need to do better'."

Why it works: skill vs will is a clean framework, and "people can fix what you name" is the line they remember.
20. How did you train people?
Say this

"Show, do, check. I show it once. They do it while I watch. Then I check their work a few days later without warning."

"And everything important was written on one page. If a rule only lives in someone's head, it dies when they take holiday."

21. How do you handle a crisis? Something on fire right now.
Say this

"Stop the bleeding first, understand it second. In a crisis I pick the one thing that hurts the customer most, fix that, and tell everyone what I chose and why."

"Panic is usually just too many priorities at the same time. Naming the first one out loud calms the room."

22. What would you do differently in that job, looking back?
Say this

"I'd have written things down earlier. I fixed a lot of problems personally before I turned any of it into a standard. That works until you're not there."

Why it works: real, small, and already fixed. Regret questions are weakness questions wearing a different hat.

3Minutes 18–28 · Your past work — engineering

They will check that the move was real and that you understand what you built. Never go deep into code unless they push — you are interviewing as a product person.

23. How did you go from operations to a tech team?Your story
Say this

"The central team had selected me to work on their projects — improving processes and training teams across countries. I was doing that alongside my local job."

"In that work we needed an internal help centre, to make the teams faster. The engineering team had no capacity, so it was going to sit in the backlog. I'd been studying development at night for a year, so I built it."

"When it shipped, they offered me a place on the central engineering team. I moved in January 2024."

"So the move was offered to me because of something I delivered. I didn't apply for it."

Why it works: "they offered it to me" is a recommendation, not a career change. And the order matters — you were already trusted centrally before you built anything. That makes the app the proof, not the reason.
Use the cross-country part elsewhere too. Training teams in other countries is evidence for three different questions: stakeholder management, working with people you don't control, and "have you worked outside your own scope?" Most candidates have nothing for those.
24. What do you build now?
Say this

"The internal platforms the business teams run on. The main one is the HR platform used by more than a thousand employees — where people request leave and do performance reviews."

"I rebuilt it from a low-code tool into a real application: Next.js on the front, NestJS and Prisma on the back, MariaDB. It was a migration of a live system people use every day, not a fresh start."

Why it works: "live system, not greenfield" is the part that impresses engineers. Anyone can build on an empty page.
25. Tell me about something you're proud of technically.The 50s → 6s story
Say this

"A screen in production took 50 seconds to load. People had stopped using it."

"I looked at the query instead of guessing. Three things: I indexed the columns we were joining on, I removed joins to tables whose columns we never showed, and I stopped selecting fields the page didn't display."

"50 seconds to 6. No new servers, no rewrite."

Why it works: number, then mechanism, then the cost. "No new servers" is the line that says you think about money, not just code.
26. Tell me about a bug you caused.Honesty test
Say this — swap in your real one

"Describe your real incident in one sentence."

"I found it, I said it immediately, and I fixed it. Then I added the check that would have caught it."

"What I took from it: the mistake costs an hour, hiding it costs a week."

Why it works: the structure is what matters — own it, fix it, prevent it, one lesson. Do not perform guilt. Calm ownership is what they are scoring.
Do not say "I can't think of one." That answer means either you don't ship, or you don't notice.
27. How do you work with other engineers?
Say this

"I ask before I assume. Most of my questions are 'what breaks if we do it this way?' — the person who built it knows, and it takes them two minutes to tell me."

"In reviews I comment on the thing, never the person, and I say what I'd do instead."

28. Are you leaving engineering behind? Won't you miss it?
Say this

"I'm not leaving it, I'm using it. A PM who can read the code and the database asks better questions and gets lied to less — not on purpose, just less."

"I won't be writing the production code. I'll be the person who knows what they're really telling me when they say it's complicated."

Why it works: it answers the real fear behind the question — will you try to do the engineers' job? — with a clear no.
29. How do you learn something new?
Say this

"I build something small with it in the first week. Reading about a thing and using a thing are different skills, and only one of them survives an interview."

"I did a full-stack diploma in a year while running the warehouse full time. That's how I learn — in parallel, with a deadline, and with something real at the end."

4Minutes 28–40 · The job itself — product

Here they test whether you understand the craft. Answers stay short and concrete. If you can say it as a list of steps, say it as a list of steps.

30. What does a product manager actually do?Memorise word for word
Say this

"Five things. Find the real problem, decide what we do first, explain it clearly, help the team ship it, and prove whether it worked."

Why it works: it is short enough to sound certain and complete enough to be right. Then stop. They will pick the one they care about and ask a follow-up — and now you are having a conversation instead of giving a speech.
31. What is the difference between a product manager and a project manager?
Say this

"A project manager owns the plan — dates, tasks, who does what. A product manager owns the outcome — was it worth building at all."

"Project manager asks: are we on time? Product manager asks: are we building the right thing?"

32. How do you find out what to build?Discovery
Say this

"Three sources. Talk to users, look at the data, and listen to the teams who face the customer every day — support, sales, operations."

"The data tells me where people drop off. The users tell me why. Neither one is enough alone."

33. How do you run a user interview?
Say this

"I ask about the past, not the future. 'What did you do last time?' not 'would you use this?'"

"People are bad at predicting what they'd use and very good at remembering what annoyed them. And I never describe my idea first — that ends the honesty."

Why it works: "past not future" is the single most respected rule in product discovery. Saying it tells them you have read the craft properly.
34. How many users do you need to talk to?
Say this

"Five to eight for a problem. By then you're hearing the same thing again, and that's the signal to stop and go build something."

35. You have 10 requests and room for 3. How do you decide?Guaranteed question
Say this

"Three questions per item. How many users does it hit, how much does it move the metric we care about, and how much work is it."

"Then I do the cheap high-impact ones first — not because they're the most exciting, but because shipping early tells you whether you were right."

"And I tell the seven people who didn't get in. Not 'no', but 'not now, and here's what would change that'."

Why it works: the third part is what separates you. Most candidates answer the maths. Prioritisation is mostly a communication job.
36. The CEO wants a feature you think is wrong. What do you do?Trap
Say this

"First I find out what problem they're actually solving. Usually the feature is their solution to something real, and the real thing is negotiable even when the feature isn't."

"Then I show the cost — what we don't ship if we ship this. Not opinion, trade-off."

"If they still want it after that, we do it. They have information I don't. But I write down what we expected, and we look at it after."

Why it works: you push back with evidence and you accept the decision. Both halves matter. A PM who never yields is a problem; a PM who never pushes back is furniture.
37. How do you say no?
Say this

"I don't say no to the person, I say no to the timing, and I show what it would push out."

"'Not this quarter, because it costs the same as the retention work' is a sentence people accept. 'No' on its own is a sentence people go around."

38. What goes in a spec?
Say this

"Five parts. The problem and who has it. What success looks like as a number. What we're building. What we're deliberately not building. And the open questions."

"The 'not building' part saves the most time. It's the one everyone skips."

39. How do you work with engineers day to day?
Say this

"I bring the problem and the constraint, not the solution. They almost always find a cheaper way than the one I had in my head."

"And I protect them from noise. If four people ask for four things in one week, that's my job to absorb, not theirs."

40. What does a good roadmap look like?
Say this

"Now, next, later. Now has dates because the work is understood. Next has a quarter. Later has no date at all — it's a list of problems, not features."

"A roadmap with exact dates twelve months out is fiction, and everyone in the room knows it."

41. How do you measure success?
Say this

"One main metric, plus one guardrail so we don't win the main one by breaking something else."

"For a learning product the main one is whether the student actually learned — not time in the app. Time in the app is easy to increase and usually means they're confused."

Why it works: the second half proves you understand the difference between a metric that looks good and a metric that means something. That is the whole job.
42. How do you know if a feature worked?
Say this

"I write down before we build it what number should move and by how much. Then I look."

"If you decide afterwards what success meant, everything is a success."

43. What do you do if a feature failed?
Say this

"Say it out loud, quickly. Then work out which part failed — the problem, the solution, or the way we told people about it. Those have three different fixes."

"Then either fix it or remove it. Leaving dead features in a product is a tax you pay forever."

44. What would your first 90 days be?Guaranteed question
Say this

"First 30 days: listen. Use the product like a real user, talk to the engineers, talk to support, read whatever data exists. No opinions yet."

"Days 30 to 60: pick one thing. Something small that clearly matters, ship it, and earn the right to touch the big things."

"Days 60 to 90: the plan. By then I know enough to say what we should do next and why, with evidence instead of guesses."

Why it works: listen, small win, then plan. It shows humility and it shows you intend to produce something in the first quarter. Both are being tested.
45. This role manages other PMs. You haven't done that.Hard
Say this

"I've managed a team — hiring conversations, training, performance, shift planning. Managing PMs is new, and the difference is that I'd be coaching decisions instead of checking work."

"What I know about managing: the job is to make the decision clear and then let them make it. My instinct will be to solve it myself. That's the thing I'd have to watch."

Why it works: you name your own risk before they do. That is the most credible thing a candidate can do in a stretch role.
46. How do you handle a stakeholder who goes around you?
Say this

"I go and ask why. Usually they went around me because I was slow or they didn't know what was happening. Both of those are mine to fix."

"A weekly update that people actually read solves most of it before it starts."

5Minutes 40–48 · The AI part

They do not expect you to be an ML engineer. They expect clear thinking and honest limits. Every definition here is one or two sentences — that is the correct length.

47. Explain an LLM in simple words.
Say this

"It's a model that predicts the next word, trained on an enormous amount of text."

"That's why it's excellent at language and unreliable on facts. It isn't looking anything up. It's producing what usually comes next."

Why it works: the second sentence is the one that matters. It explains every problem you will discuss for the next ten minutes.
48. What is a hallucination and what do you do about it?
Say this

"It's when the model invents something and says it confidently. It's not a bug you fix, it's a behaviour you design around."

"Three things: give it the source material instead of relying on memory, let it say 'I don't know', and show the student where the answer came from so they can check."

Do not say "you fix it with a better prompt." Prompts reduce it. Nothing removes it.
49. What is RAG?Guaranteed question
Say this

"Search first, then answer. When a student asks a question, we find the right pages from our own content, put them in the prompt, and tell the model to answer only from those pages."

"It's the difference between an open-book exam and a memory test."

Why it works: the open-book line makes a non-technical interviewer nod and a technical one relax. Use it.
50. Why RAG instead of just asking the model?
Say this

"Three reasons. It answers from our curriculum, not the internet's average. We can update the content without retraining anything. And we can show the source, which matters when the user is a student."

51. When would you fine-tune instead?
Say this

"Fine-tuning teaches style and format. RAG gives facts. If the model keeps answering in the wrong tone or the wrong shape, fine-tune. If it gets the content wrong, that's a retrieval problem, and fine-tuning won't help."

52. How do you know if the AI is any good?Evals
Say this

"You need a test set. A few hundred real questions with answers we agree are correct. Every time we change the prompt or the model, we run it and compare."

"Without that you're changing a prompt and guessing. I've seen the same pattern in operations — if you don't measure before and after, every change feels like an improvement."

Why it works: evals are the thing most candidates forget and most AI teams are desperate for. Bringing it up unprompted is a strong signal.
53. How do you control the cost?
Say this

"Small model first, big model only for the hard cases. Cache the answers to questions hundreds of students ask the same way. Keep prompts short — you pay per word, both directions."

"And measure cost per active student, not total spend. Total spend going up is fine if the number per student is going down."

54. What are AI agents, and would you use them here?
Say this

"An agent is the model planning several steps and using tools instead of answering once."

"More power, more ways to go wrong. I'd use it where a wrong step is cheap — building a study plan you can edit. Not for grading. A wrong grade is expensive and a student remembers it."

55. How would you build an AI tutor?The signature question
Say this

"Four pieces. First, the content — our curriculum, in small chunks, searchable. Second, the student model — what this person already knows and where they struggle. Third, the tutor itself — it asks questions instead of giving answers, because a student who gets the answer learns nothing. Fourth, the check — does the student actually score better afterwards."

"The hard part isn't the model. It's the fourth one."

Why it works: four short pieces, then a judgement. "The hard part is measuring learning" is the sentence a Product Lead says and an enthusiast doesn't.
56. How do you know a student has actually learned something?
Say this

"Not from one right answer — that can be luck or copying. I'd want it right several times, spaced out over days, and on a question shaped differently from the one they practised."

"Remembering tomorrow is learning. Remembering thirty seconds later is short-term memory."

57. What would you personalise?
Say this

"Difficulty and order. Give them the next thing they're ready for, not the next thing on the list."

"I'd be careful with personalising too much too early. With little data you're mostly personalising noise, and the student feels it as randomness."

58. Arabic content — what's the challenge?Your edge
Say this

"Two things. The models are weaker in Arabic than in English, so anything you measured in English you have to measure again. And there's a product decision: students speak dialect, school is in Modern Standard Arabic."

"My answer: understand dialect, reply in Modern Standard. Meet them where they type, teach them what they're examined on."

"I speak Arabic, French and English, so I can read the failures myself instead of waiting for a report."

Why it works: a clear decision with a reason, and a personal advantage nobody else in the process has. This may be your strongest 30 seconds in the whole hour.
59. The AI teaches something wrong. What now?
Say this

"Same as a wrong product reaching a customer. Stop it, find how many people got it, fix the source, and add the check that would have caught it."

"And there needs to be a report button on every answer. Users will find your errors faster than any test set."

60. Students are minors. What about their data?
Say this

"Collect the minimum, keep it short, and never send a child's name or personal details to an outside model."

"Parents should be able to see what's stored and delete it. With minors, the safe answer and the right answer are the same answer."

61. What worries you about AI in education?
Say this

"That it gets the answer for them. It's very easy to build something students love and learn nothing from, because struggling is the part that teaches."

"So the tutor should hint first, ask a question back, and only give the answer at the end."

62. How much of this have you actually built?Honesty test
Say this

"I've built with these tools myself, not in production at scale. I want to be clear about that."

"What I bring is the rest of it — knowing which problem is worth solving, measuring whether it worked, and shipping with a team. The AI part I've learned and I'm still learning."

Why it works: they can tell. A clean admission followed by what you do bring is strong. An exaggeration that collapses under one follow-up question ends the interview.

6Minutes 48–56 · The psychology questions

These are not small talk. They are testing self-awareness, honesty and how you behave when things are bad. The rule for all of them: be specific, be short, and never sound rehearsed even though you are.

63. What is your biggest strength?
Say this

"I finish things. I ran a warehouse full time and finished a development diploma in the same year. Nobody asked me to do that. I just don't leave things half done."

Why it works: one word, then proof. A strength without evidence is a personality quiz answer.
64. What is your biggest weakness?Trap
Say this

"I go too deep. I like detail, so I can spend an hour on something worth ten minutes."

"What I do now is ask what decision the detail changes. If it changes nothing, I stop. It works, but I have to do it on purpose — it isn't natural yet."

Why it works: real weakness, real fix, honest about the fix being a work in progress. The honesty in that last line is what makes them believe the whole answer.
Never say: "I'm a perfectionist", "I work too hard", "I care too much". Everyone says them and everyone hears them as "I have nothing to tell you".
65. How do you handle stress and pressure?
Say this

"I get quieter and more organised, not louder. Peak season taught me that."

"When everything is urgent I write the list, pick the first thing, and say out loud what I'm not doing. Most stress is just too many priorities at once."

66. Tell me about a time you failed.STAR
Say this — use your real story

"Your real failure, one sentence: what you tried and what went wrong."

"What went wrong was mine: the specific decision you got wrong."

"What I changed: the thing you do differently now. I've done it that way since."

Pick a real one and pick it now. Rules for choosing: it must be genuinely yours, it must be finished, it must not have destroyed anything, and you must have changed something because of it. A failure with no change is just a story about bad luck.
Do not choose a fake failure ("I was too ambitious"). Do not blame a colleague, a manager, or a supplier. One sentence of blame cancels the whole answer.
67. Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.
Say this

"I disagree in private, with data, once. If they still decide the other way, I do it properly — not half-heartedly while waiting to be proved right."

"In the warehouse that happened over a priority call. I gave my numbers, the decision went the other way, and I executed it. We looked at the result afterwards and adjusted."

Why it works: "and then I did it properly" is the part they are listening for. Lots of candidates prove they can argue. Few prove they can commit after losing.
68. How do you take criticism?
Say this

"I ask for the example. 'Be more strategic' I can't use. 'In that meeting you went into detail too early' I can fix tomorrow."

"I don't defend in the moment. I take it, think about it, and come back if I disagree."

69. What motivates you?
Say this

"Seeing something I built being used. The help centre app is still in use by the agents. That feeling is why I moved into building things in the first place."

Do not say "challenges" or "learning" alone. Everyone says it and it means nothing without an example.
70. What demotivates you?
Say this

"Work that doesn't ship. I'd rather ship something small and imperfect than write a perfect document that sits in a folder."

71. Where do you see yourself in five years?
Say this

"Leading a product area, with a team, in something that has real users. Honestly, five years ago I wouldn't have predicted this conversation — so I plan two years ahead and stay open after that."

Why it works: ambitious and honest. Pretending you have a ten-year plan sounds rehearsed; saying you have none sounds passive. Two years is the right answer.
72. What would your current manager say about you?
Say this

"That I take things off his plate without being asked, and that I ask a lot of questions about why before I start. He'd probably say the second one is sometimes tiring."

Why it works: the small self-criticism at the end is what makes the compliment believable.
73. What would your team say about you?
Say this

"That I'm clear and I don't leave them guessing. And that I'm demanding about the standard but I never make it personal."

74. Do you prefer working alone or in a team?
Say this

"I think alone and I decide with others. I need quiet to look at the data properly, but a decision made alone in a corner is usually wrong somewhere."

75. How do you handle ambiguity? Nobody tells you what to do.
Say this

"I'm comfortable with it — that was the warehouse most days. I pick the smallest useful thing I can do without permission, do it, and show it. It's much easier to get direction from people once there's something in front of them."

76. What do you do when you don't know something?Character question
Say this

"I say I don't know, and then I say when I'll come back with the answer."

"In operations, guessing out loud costs real money. I'd rather be the person who is sometimes slow than the person who is sometimes wrong and confident."

Why it works: and then you must actually do it during the interview. If one question catches you, use this answer for real. It scores higher than a weak improvised answer.
77. Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news.
Say this

"Early and directly. A date we were going to miss, or stock we'd damaged."

"I give the news first, then the reason, then what I'm doing about it. In that order. People can handle bad news. What they can't handle is finding out late."

78. Tell me about a time you changed your mind.
Say this

"With the returns work. I was sure the problem was delivery, because that's what everyone said. The data said listings and packaging."

"I was wrong, and I said so before I presented the fix. That's easier than defending the old opinion and it makes people trust the new one."

79. Are you a leader or a doer?
Say this

"Both, and I know which one a company your size needs. I've led a team, and I still do the work myself when the work needs doing. I'm not looking for a job where I only send emails."

80. How do you organise your week?
Say this

"Three things that matter, written down on Monday. Everything else fits around them."

"If Friday comes and those three aren't done, the week failed, even if I was busy every hour."

81. You work remotely. How do we know you're working?
Say this

"By what I ship and by a written update nobody has to ask for. I've worked remotely on a central team since January 2024 with colleagues in another country."

"Remote works when people write things down. I write things down."

82. Why should we hire you?Close hard
Say this

"Because I've done the three things this job needs, just not under this title. I've owned an operation with real consequences. I've built the reporting people decided on. And I've shipped software with a team."

"Most candidates have one of those. I want this one, and I'll work harder for it than someone who's had the title for eight years."

Why it works: it is the only moment you are allowed to be openly competitive. Say it with a straight face and stop.
83. Why should we NOT hire you?Stress question
Say this

"If you need someone who has already run a product organisation of this size, that isn't me, and you'd find out in month two."

"If you want someone who learns fast, works hard, and already understands your users and your engineers — then it is."

Why it works: you answer it straight instead of dodging, and you finish on your side of the argument. Dodging this one is the failure.
84. What do you do outside work?
Say this

"Two real things you actually do. And I build small side projects — I built my own CV and interview preparation app, which is how I ended up so ready for this conversation."

Why it works: it is true, it is slightly funny, and it proves the "I build things" claim one more time without sounding like you are proving anything.
85. Tell me something that isn't on your CV.
Say this

"That for a whole year I was doing two full-time things at once — the warehouse in the day, the development diploma at night. The CV shows both, but it doesn't show that they overlapped."

86. How do you handle boring work?
Say this

"I do it, and then I try to make it smaller. Most of my reporting work started as somebody's boring manual task."

87. Have you ever broken a rule at work?Careful
Say this

"I've gone outside my scope. Building that help centre app wasn't my job and nobody asked me to."

"Rules about safety, money and customer data I don't touch. Rules about whose job it is, I'll question if the thing needs doing."

Why it works: it gives them initiative without giving them a compliance risk. The second sentence is the one that keeps you safe.
88. What makes you angry at work?
Say this

"People knowing about a problem and saying nothing until it's too big to fix quietly. That's the one thing I'm strict about with a team."

Do not name a type of person ("lazy people", "bad managers"). Name a behaviour. Naming people sounds like you are difficult.
89. How do you build trust with a new team?
Say this

"Do the unglamorous thing first. In a warehouse that means being there at the hard hour. On a product team it means taking the annoying task nobody wants in week one."

"Then be predictable. Say what you'll do and do it, small things included."

90. This is a stretch role. Won't you struggle?Direct
Say this

"Yes, at the start. Every role I've had here was a stretch at the start — including the one where I was handed a warehouse at 24 and the one where I was handed production code two years ago."

"I've been the person who is behind and catches up before. I know what that costs and I'm willing to pay it."

Why it works: "yes, and here's my track record of exactly that" beats any denial. Never answer this with "no".
91. Are you interviewing anywhere else?
Say this

"I'm in conversation with a couple of others, nothing decided. This is the one I'm most interested in, because of the reason you gave earlier."

Why it works: honest, and it creates gentle time pressure without a threat. Never invent a fake offer — it is easy to check and it ends badly.
92. They stay silent after your answer.The silence trap
Do this

Nothing. Wait. Count to five in your head. If it goes past that, say: "Happy to go deeper on any part of that."

Why it works: some interviewers use silence on purpose to see if you talk yourself backwards. Most candidates fill it with weaker material that cancels the good answer. Be comfortable in it — it makes you look senior.

7Minutes 56–60 · Money, logistics and your questions

Do not relax here. People lose offers in the last five minutes by sounding uncertain about money or by having no questions.

93. What are your salary expectations?Say a number
Say this

"I'm looking at X to Y, depending on the whole package. What range did you have in mind for the role?"

Why it works: a range, then the question back. Whoever speaks the number second has less power, so give a range you are happy with and hand it back immediately.
Do not say "I'm flexible" or "whatever is standard". It sounds unsure and it guarantees you get the bottom of their band. Decide your number before the interview, out loud, in your kitchen.
94. That's above our range. / Can you go lower?
Say this

"I understand. What's the top of your range? If we're close, the rest of the package can bridge it — remote days, learning budget, review date."

Why it works: you don't drop your number, you widen the conversation. And you never say yes to a number in the room. "Let me see it in writing" is always correct.
95. What's your current salary?
Say this

"I'd rather talk about the value of this role than what an internal engineering role pays. My expectation is X to Y."

96. When could you start? / Notice period?
Say this

"X weeks notice. I'll leave properly — I'd want to hand over cleanly, and you'd want the same from me one day."

97. Remote, hybrid, relocation?
Say this

"Your honest answer. I've worked remotely with a team in another country since January 2024, so I know how to make it work — and I'm happy to travel for the moments that matter, like planning and onboarding."

98. Do you have any questions for us?Never say no
Ask three of these — not more

"What does success look like for this person in the first six months?"

"What's the hardest problem in the product right now?"

"How do you decide what goes in the roadmap today?"

"What happens when the AI gets something wrong — who finds out, and how?"

"What would make you say in a year that this hire was a mistake?"

Why it works: every one of them is about the job, not about you. The last one is brave and it often gets you the most useful answer of the whole hour — it tells you exactly what to address in your closing statement.
Do not ask about holidays, working hours or promotion timing in the first interview. Those come after the offer.
99. Anything you'd like to add before we finish?Your closing
Say this — 20 seconds, then stop

"Just one thing. I know I'm not the standard profile for this role. What I'd ask you to weigh is that I've run the operation, built the reporting, and shipped the software — and I understand your users in their own language."

"I want this one. If you give it to me, I'll be the person who does the unglamorous part."

Why it works: it is the last thing they hear, so it should be the argument you most want them to repeat to each other after you leave. Practise it until it comes out in one breath.

8The bonus round — questions that catch people out

Less common, but they happen. All of them are testing thinking, not knowledge.

100. Teach me something in two minutes.
Say this

Teach them how a return actually works, or what an index does to a slow query. Something you have done with your hands. Start with why it matters, then one example, then the rule.

Why it works: for an education product, how you teach is part of the assessment. Pick something you know so well you could explain it while tired.
101. How many students in Tunisia would use this?Estimation
Say this — they want the method, not the answer

"Let me work from population. Roughly 12 million people. School age is maybe a fifth of that. Of those, the ones with a smartphone and a reason to pay, maybe a quarter. So the ceiling is hundreds of thousands, and the realistic first market is much smaller."

"Tell me if you'd cut it differently — the numbers are rough, the method is the point."

Why it works: say your assumptions out loud and do the arithmetic slowly. Being wrong with a clear method passes. Being right with a guess fails.
102. Our main metric dropped 20% yesterday. Go.
Say this

"First: is it real or is it the tracking? Half of these are a broken event."

"If it's real: is it everywhere, or one platform, one country, one user type? Did we release something? Did something outside change — a holiday, exams, a competitor?"

"Then narrow it down and fix the thing, not the number."

103. Pick a product you like and tell me how you'd improve it.
Say this

"I'd use a product you genuinely use daily. Who it's for, what it's great at, the one moment it frustrates me, and what I'd change first — and how I'd know the change worked."

Prepare this one for real. Write it out before the interview. Five sentences. Do not pick their product for this question — pick it for the "what do you know about us" question instead.
104. What's a product you think is badly made?
Say this

"Name one, briefly. But I'd say what's hard about it rather than that it's bad — most bad products are a good team with a constraint I can't see."

Why it works: critical but generous. Interviewers watch carefully for how you talk about other people's work, because one day that will be their team's work.
105. Sell me this product in 30 seconds.
Say this — ask first

"Before I do — who am I selling to? A student, a parent, or a school? Because those are three different answers."

Why it works: asking who the customer is before pitching is the correct answer. Then give the 30 seconds.
106. What's the biggest risk in this product?
Say this

"That students enjoy it and don't learn. It's the risk with every AI education product, because engagement is easy to measure and learning is hard to measure."

"Which is why I'd want a real outcome measure early, even a rough one."

107. Do you have any concerns about the role?
Say this

"One honest one. I'd want to know how much of this job is building and how much is reporting upward. I'm at my best close to the product and the users."

Why it works: a real question shows you are evaluating them too, which raises your value. "No concerns at all" makes you sound desperate for any job.
108. What if we offer you a lower level than this one?
Say this

"I'd listen. What matters to me is the scope and a clear path — if the title is smaller but I own a real problem, that's a conversation. I'd want to know what would need to be true to move up, and when we'd look at it."

109. You seem to change roles a lot. Are you going to stay?
Say this

"I've been at one company for seven years. I changed roles inside it, twice, because they asked me to."

"That's the opposite of moving around."

Why it works: short, factual, and slightly firm. Some questions deserve a correction rather than an apology.
110. Is there anything we haven't asked that we should have?
Say this

"You haven't asked me what I'd do in the first week if you hired me. It's the thing I've thought about most."

Why it works: you get to give one more prepared answer, of your own choosing, right at the end. Have it ready.
111. You blank completely. You have nothing.Emergency
Say this

"I want to give you a real answer rather than a general one — can I come back to that at the end?"

Why it works: it is calm, it is professional, and interviewers almost always say yes. Then actually come back to it. Most people never do, and coming back is what they remember.
112. They cut the interview short after 20 minutes.
Do this

Don't panic and don't read it as bad. Ask one good question, give your 20-second closing statement, and send the follow-up email the same day.

Why it works: short interviews happen for a hundred reasons that have nothing to do with you. The closing statement is the part you control, so use it whatever the length.

9The mock — a real 60 minutes, start to finish

Read this out loud, both parts, once. This is where you learn to handle the follow-up — which is where most interviews are actually won or lost.

Minute 1 — opening
Them

Thanks for making time. So — tell me about yourself.

You

"I'm Syrine. I spent my first five years at Jumia Group with the local team, running end-to-end fulfillment. That's where I learned to own outcomes — I defined the KPIs and built the reporting management used to plan and decide."

"Then the central team selected me, so I was half local and half central — projects to improve the processes and train teams across countries. In that work we needed an internal help centre, engineering had no capacity, so I built it."

"When it shipped they offered me a place with the central engineering team, delivering the internal platforms the business teams run on. I moved in January 2024. So I've been on both sides — the team that feels the problem, and the team that builds the fix."

Them

And you want to move into product. Why?

You

"Because the part I'm best at is the part before the code — deciding what's actually worth building. I did that in the warehouse every day, and now I can also build it. Product is both."

Minute 6 — the objection, early
Them

I'll be direct. The role says eight years of product management. You have zero.

You

"That's fair and I won't argue with it. So let me tell you what you get instead."

"Someone who's owned a live operation with real money and real customers. Someone who built the reporting people made decisions on. And someone who can sit with engineers and understand what they're actually telling me, because I am one."

"The title I don't have. The judgement underneath it I've been doing for five years."

Them

Give me an example of that judgement.

You

"Returns. Everyone treated them as normal cost. I started coding every return with a reason — wrong item, damaged, listing mismatch, customer refused."

"After a few weeks it was a list instead of noise. A small number of sellers drove a big share, and most of those were listing problems, not delivery problems. So we fixed listings, tightened packaging for those sellers, escalated the ones who didn't improve."

"Return rate went from X to Y."

Them

How did you know the reason codes were accurate? People tick the fastest box.

You

"They do. I did the first few weeks myself so I knew what the categories should be. Then I wrote the rules on one page, trained the team, and audited a sample every week."

"It wasn't perfect. It was consistent, which is what you need for a trend."

Minute 20 — the craft
Them

What does a product manager do, in your words?

You

"Five things. Find the real problem, decide what we do first, explain it clearly, help the team ship it, and prove whether it worked."

Them

Ten requests, capacity for three. Walk me through it.

You

"Three questions each. How many users does it hit, how much does it move the metric we care about, how much work is it. Cheap and high impact first — not because it's exciting, but because shipping early tells you if you were right."

"Then I tell the seven people who didn't make it. Not 'no' — 'not now, and here's what would change that'."

Them

One of those seven is the CEO and they're not happy.

You

"Then I find out what problem they're solving, because the feature is usually their solution to something real, and the real thing is negotiable even when the feature isn't."

"I show what we don't ship if we ship this. If they still want it, we do it — they have information I don't. But I write down what we expected, and we look at it after."

Minute 34 — AI
Them

Explain RAG to me like I'm not technical.

You

"Search first, then answer. The student asks something, we find the right pages from our own content, put them in the prompt, and tell the model to answer only from those pages."

"It's an open-book exam instead of a memory test."

Them

And when it still gets it wrong?

You

"Then I want to know which half failed — did we retrieve the wrong pages, or did we retrieve the right ones and it answered badly. Those have different fixes and people confuse them constantly."

"And there's a report button on every answer. Users find your errors faster than any test set."

Them

How much of this have you actually built yourself?

You

"I've built with these tools myself, not in production at scale, and I want to be clear about that."

"What I bring is the rest — knowing which problem is worth solving, measuring whether it worked, and shipping with a team. The AI part I've learned and I'm still learning."

Minute 44 — the person
Them

What's your biggest weakness?

You

"I go too deep. I like detail, so I can spend an hour on something worth ten minutes."

"What I do now is ask what decision the detail changes. If it changes nothing, I stop. It works — but I have to do it on purpose. It isn't natural yet."

Them

This job would be a stretch. Won't you struggle?

You

"Yes, at the start. Every role I've had was a stretch at the start — including the one where I was handed a warehouse, and the one where I was handed production code."

"I've been behind and caught up before. I know what it costs and I'm willing to pay it."

Minute 54 — money and close
Them

What are you expecting on salary?

You

"I'm looking at X to Y, depending on the whole package. What range did you have in mind for the role?"

Them

That's at the top of our band. Any questions for us?

You

"Three. What does success look like for this person in the first six months? What's the hardest problem in the product right now? And — what would make you say in a year that this hire was a mistake?"

Them

Good question. Probably if they stayed theoretical and didn't ship. Anything to add before we close?

You

"Just one thing, and it answers what you just said. I'm not the standard profile for this role. What I'd ask you to weigh is that I've run the operation, built the reporting, and shipped the software — and I understand your users in their own language."

"I want this one. If you give it to me, I'll be the person who does the unglamorous part."

10After the interview

The follow-up email — send it within 24 hours

Subject: Thank you — Role

Hi Name,

Thank you for your time today. I enjoyed the conversation,
especially the one specific thing you discussed.

One thing I kept thinking about afterwards: one short,
useful idea about their product — two sentences, no more.

I'm very interested in the role. Happy to answer anything else.

Best,
Syrine Larbi
Why it works: the middle paragraph is the whole email. A thank-you note is polite and forgettable. A thank-you note with one useful idea in it is a second interview. Keep it under 100 words.

If they say no

Reply with this

"Thank you for telling me. Could you share the one thing that would have made the difference? I'd like to be a stronger candidate next time — and I'd be glad to stay in touch if something else opens up."

Why it works: people answer that question honestly surprisingly often, and it is the most valuable feedback you will ever get. It also keeps you on the list. Roles reopen.

The ten rules for the room

  • 1. Answer, give one example, stop. Never past 90 seconds.
  • 2. Say "I", not "we". They cannot score "we".
  • 3. Number first, then how you did it. A number without a mechanism sounds invented.
  • 4. Never say anything negative about Jumia, a manager, or a colleague.
  • 5. "I don't know, I'll find out" is a strong answer. Guessing confidently is a weak one.
  • 6. Agree with a fair objection before you answer it. Arguing makes it bigger.
  • 7. Never apologise for your English, your CV, or your background.
  • 8. Let silence sit. Count to five before you fill it.
  • 9. Ask three questions at the end. Never zero.
  • 10. Use your closing statement whatever happens. It is the last thing they hear.
