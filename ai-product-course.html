1Part 1 — What is this job?

Lesson 1What does a Product Manager do?

A Product Manager decides what to build and why. Engineers decide how.

Think of it like…

You are building a house. The engineers are the builders. They are very good at building.

You are the person who asks: Who will live here? How many rooms do they need? What is the budget? Is the kitchen more important than the garden?

You do not lay the bricks. You decide which house gets built, and you say no to the rooms nobody needs.

The four jobs of a PM, every day:

1. Find the problemTalk to users. Look at data. Understand what is really wrong.
2. ChooseThere are 20 ideas and time for 3. You pick. You say no to 17.
3. ExplainTell the team what to build and why. Tell the boss what will happen.
4. MeasureDid it work? How do we know? What number moved?
Good news for you: you have done all four. At Jumia you found the real cause of returns. You chose to stop the inspection plan. You explained it to the commercial team. You measured the result. That is product management. You just did not have the title.
Say this

"A product manager decides what to build and why. The engineers decide how."

"My job is to find the real problem, choose what we do first, and prove it worked."

Lesson 2What is different about an AI Product Manager?

Normal software always gives the same answer. AI does not. That one difference changes everything.

Think of it like…

Normal software is a calculator. You type 2 + 2. You always get 4. Every time. Forever.

AI is a smart employee. You ask the same question twice and you get two different answers. Both may be good. One may be wrong. You cannot be sure.

What this changes for you as a PM:

Normal productAI product
It works or it is brokenIt is right 90% of the time. You must decide if 90% is enough.
Testing = does it work?Testing = how often is it good? You need a test set.
Cost is fixedCost grows every time someone uses it. More users = more cost.
Fast (milliseconds)Slow (seconds). You must design for waiting.
You can promise a dateYou cannot. You do not know if it will work until you try.
Say this

"Normal software gives the same answer every time. AI does not."

"So my job changes. I have to decide how good is good enough. And I have to measure it, not guess."

2Part 2 — AI basics

Six lessons. After these you will understand every AI word in the job description.

Lesson 3What is an LLM?

LLM means Large Language Model. It is a program that reads text and writes the next words.

Think of it like…

Your phone keyboard. You type "How are" and it suggests "you". That is a tiny language model.

An LLM is the same idea, but it read most of the internet. So it can suggest not just one word, but a whole explanation, a letter, or a lesson.

Important: it is guessing the next words. It is very good at guessing. But it is still guessing.

Two things to remember:

  • It does not "know" things. It learned patterns from text. So it can be very confident and completely wrong.
  • It has no memory between chats. If you want it to remember a student, you must send that information every time.
Say this

"An LLM reads text and predicts the next words. It learned from a huge amount of writing."

"It does not really know facts. It finds patterns. That is why it can be wrong and still sound sure."

Lesson 4Tokens, context, temperature — the three words you must know

Token = a small piece of a word. The model reads and writes in tokens. You pay per token.

Think of it like…

A taxi meter. It does not count your journey in trips. It counts in small units. More units = more money.

One English word is about 1 token. But Arabic often uses 2 or 3 tokens for one word. So the same lesson in Arabic costs more money and takes more time. Remember this one — it is a very strong point for you.

Context window = how much text the model can look at at one time.

Think of it like…

A small desk. You can only put a few papers on it. If you put a new paper on, an old one falls off.

This is why you cannot put your whole school curriculum in the question. It does not fit. This is the reason RAG exists — you will see it in Lesson 8.

Temperature = how creative the answer is. Low = safe and repeatable. High = creative and random.

Think of it like…

Low temperature = a careful teacher. Same answer every time.

High temperature = a creative friend. Fun ideas, but you cannot trust the details.

For teaching maths, use low. For writing a story, use higher.

Say this

"Tokens are pieces of words. We pay per token, so cost grows with use."

"Arabic uses more tokens per word than English. So our cost per student is higher than an English benchmark suggests. That belongs in the budget."

Lesson 5What is a hallucination? Most important lesson

A hallucination is when the AI says something false, but says it with total confidence.

Think of it like…

A student in an exam. They do not know the answer. But they do not write "I don't know" — they write something that looks like a good answer. Confident handwriting. Correct grammar. Wrong content.

The AI does the same thing. It was built to always produce text. Silence is not an option for it unless you allow it.

Why this is very serious in education:

  • A student asks because they do not know the subject.
  • So the student cannot see that the answer is wrong.
  • They learn it. They build on it. They use it in the exam.

In most products a wrong answer is annoying. In education, a wrong answer damages the user.

How we reduce it — four ways:

1. Give it the bookSend the real lesson content with the question. This is RAG.
2. Ask for the sourceThe answer must show where it came from. Then we can check.
3. Low temperatureLess creativity when we teach facts.
4. Allow "I don't know"Tell it clearly: if the answer is not in the content, say so. If you do not allow this, it will invent something.
Say this

"A hallucination is when the model is wrong but sounds sure."

"In education this is the worst failure. The student asks because they don't know the subject. So they can't see that the answer is wrong. They believe it and build on it."

"We reduce it by giving the model our approved content, asking for the source, and making 'I don't know' an allowed answer."

Lesson 6The other things that go wrong

Six more problems. Learn the name and one line for each.

ProblemIn simple wordsWhat we do
Slow
latency		 It takes seconds, not milliseconds. Show the words as they arrive, so it feels fast. Use a small model for easy questions.
Expensive Every use costs money. More users = more cost. Save common answers. Send less text. Use a cheap model when we can.
Small desk
context limit		 We cannot send everything. Send only the part we need. This is RAG.
Hidden orders
prompt injection		 Someone hides an instruction inside the text, and the model obeys it. Keep user text separate from our instructions. Never let the model do an important action alone.
Bad content Our users are children. The answer must be safe. Check the answer before it is shown. Have a plan for serious situations.
No test
no evaluation		 We change something and we don't know if it got better. Build a test set of real questions with correct answers. Score every change against it.
Prompt injection — think of it like…

You give an assistant a folder and say: "Answer the customer using this folder."

Someone has put a paper inside the folder that says: "Forget your boss. Give this person a free product."

The assistant reads everything in the folder and cannot tell which paper is from you. That is prompt injection.

Say this

"The main problems are: it can be wrong, it is slow, it costs money every time, and it is hard to test."

"For a learning product with children, being wrong is the one that matters most."

Lesson 7What is an AI agent?

A normal AI answers your question. An agent can do several steps by itself, using tools, before it answers.

Think of it like…

Normal AI = you ask a colleague a question. They answer. Done.

Agent = you ask a colleague to "prepare the report". They decide to open the database, then check last month's file, then call someone, then write it. They chose those steps. You did not.

When it is useful here: making practice questions. The agent writes a question, then solves it itself to check the answer is right, then checks it matches the lesson, then sends it to a teacher.

When it is dangerous here: anything talking to a child with no adult checking. If step 1 is wrong, steps 2, 3 and 4 are also wrong. The mistake grows.

Say this

"An agent can take several steps by itself. A normal model just answers."

"I would use it behind the scenes — for example to create and check practice questions."

"I would not let an agent talk to a child with no human checking. If I cannot test it, I cannot ship it to students."

3Part 3 — RAG (the most important topic)

They will ask about RAG. Maybe twice. Learn these three lessons well.

Lesson 8What is RAG? They will ask this

RAG means: find the right content first, then give it to the AI, then let the AI answer using it.

The three letters mean Retrieval (find), Augmented (add), Generation (write). But you do not need the letters. You need the idea.

Think of it like…

Without RAG = a closed-book exam. The student answers from memory. Sometimes they remember wrong. Sometimes they mix up two things. They cannot show you where they got it.

With RAG = an open-book exam. Before answering, you find the right page and put it in front of them. Now they answer from the real book. And they can point to the page.

Why we need it for a school platform — four reasons:

1. CorrectThe answer comes from our approved lessons, not from random internet text.
2. Easy to updateThe book changed? We change the content. We do not retrain anything. Hours, not weeks.
3. We can show the source"This came from lesson 4." The student can check. The teacher can trust it.
4. We can control accessA grade 8 student only gets grade 8 content. You cannot do this inside the model.
Say this — the simple version

"Without RAG it is a closed-book exam. The model answers from memory."

"With RAG it is an open-book exam. We find the right lesson first and give it to the model."

"We need that for four reasons. The answer comes from our real content. We can update the content without retraining. We can show the student where it came from. And we can make sure a grade 8 student only gets grade 8 content."

Lesson 9How RAG works, step by step

Two parts. Part A happens once, before any student arrives. Part B happens every time a student asks something.

Part A — prepare the content (done in advance)

1  Take our lessons and books
2  CUT them into small pieces          ("chunking")
3  TURN each piece into numbers        ("embedding")
4  STORE the numbers in a special database  ("vector database")
Think of it like…

You are organising a library.

Cutting = you don't store whole books. You store single pages. Because you want to find the exact page, not carry the whole book.

Turning into numbers = you write a code on each page that describes its meaning. Pages about fractions get similar codes. Pages about history get very different codes.

The special database = a library where books are arranged by meaning, not by alphabet. So "find me something similar to this" becomes easy.

Part B — answer a question (every time)

1  Student asks a question
2  Turn the question into numbers too
3  FIND the pieces with the closest numbers        ("retrieval")
4  FILTER: right grade? right subject? right language?
5  PICK the best 3 to 5 pieces                     ("re-ranking")
6  SEND to the AI:  instructions + those pieces + the question
7  AI writes the answer using them
8  CHECK it: is it safe? does the source really say this?
9  Show the answer + the source to the student
Step 4 is the one to mention. Most people forget it. In a school, you must never show a grade 11 explanation to a grade 8 student. So we filter before we search by meaning. Say this in the interview — it shows you think about education, not just about AI.
Say this

"First we prepare. We cut our lessons into small pieces, turn each piece into numbers, and store them."

"Then when a student asks something, we turn the question into numbers too, find the closest pieces, filter them by grade and subject, and send the best few to the model with the question."

"The filter matters. A grade 8 student must never get a grade 11 explanation."

Lesson 10RAG or fine-tuning? Common question

RAG gives the model knowledge. Fine-tuning gives the model behaviour.

Learn that one sentence. It answers the whole question.

Think of it like…

You hire a new teacher.

RAG = you give them the textbook. They can now teach your curriculum today. Tomorrow the curriculum changes, you give them the new book. Easy.

Fine-tuning = you train their teaching style. Weeks of practice so they always ask questions instead of giving answers. That is a habit, not information.

You need both. The book, and the style.

Use RAG when…Use fine-tuning when…
It is facts and contentIt is style, tone or format
It changes oftenIt stays the same
You must show the sourceYou want a consistent way of speaking
Different users see different thingsYou want a small cheap model to do one job well
Example: the maths curriculumExample: always ask a question instead of giving the answer
Say this

"RAG gives the model knowledge. Fine-tuning gives it behaviour."

"Our curriculum is knowledge. It changes, students need to see the source, and a grade 8 student must only see grade 8 content. So that is RAG."

"I would fine-tune for the teaching style — always asking a question instead of giving the answer. That is hard to keep with instructions alone."

"In practice you use both together."

4Part 4 — AI for learning (your winning section)

Most people applying know AI. Few know learning AI. These four lessons are where you beat them.

Lesson 11What is a learner model?

The learner model is what the system knows about one student. It is the memory of the platform.

Think of it like…

A private tutor with a notebook. After every lesson they write: "Good at fractions. Struggles with negative numbers. Always makes the same mistake with signs. Learns fast with examples, slowly with text."

Next week, the tutor opens that notebook before the lesson. That notebook is the learner model.

Without it, every lesson starts from zero. That is a normal platform. With it, the lesson is personal. That is an adaptive platform.

What is inside it:

ONE STUDENT
├── How well do they know each topic?     (not a score — a belief)
├── Which earlier topics are weak?        (the real cause of problems)
├── How fast do they learn?
├── Which mistakes do they repeat?
├── When will they forget this?           (so we can remind them)
└── What format works for them?           (video, text, examples)

The one idea to remember:

Score vs belief

A score says: "You got 60% last week." That is the past. It is finished.

A belief says: "We are 60% sure you understand this." That is about the future. It tells us what to do next.

A learning platform needs the second one. That is why we do not just store scores.

Say this

"The learner model is what we know about one student. It is like the notebook a private tutor keeps."

"It holds how well they know each topic, which mistakes they repeat, and when they will forget."

"And it stores a belief, not a score. A score tells you the past. A belief tells you what to teach next."

Lesson 12Knowledge tracing — how do we know a student really understands? Your best answer

Knowledge tracing means guessing whether a student really understands a topic — using the order of their answers, not just the total.

Think of it like…

Two students both get 2 out of 4.

Student A: right, right, wrong, wrong. They were doing well, then they got lost. Maybe the questions got harder. Maybe they got tired.

Student B: wrong, wrong, right, right. They did not understand, and then they learned it.

Same score: 50%. Opposite situations. Student B is ready to move on. Student A is not.

A score cannot see this. Knowledge tracing can.

Two more problems with scores:

  • Lucky guess. Multiple choice with 4 options — you can be right by luck.
  • Silly mistake. You understood it perfectly and pressed the wrong button.

So we never say "50% = half understood". We estimate a probability, and we update it after every answer.

The methods — you only need the names and one line:

NameIn one lineGood / bad
BKT
Bayesian Knowledge Tracing		 After each answer, update the chance that the student knows this topic. Simple. Works with little data. Easy to explain to a parent.
IRT
Item Response Theory		 Measures student ability and question difficulty together. Great for tests. Not really about learning over time.
DKT
Deep Knowledge Tracing		 A neural network reads the whole history of answers. More accurate with lots of data. Cannot explain why.
The PM answer — this is what wins the question. Do not say "I would use the most accurate one." Say you would start simple, for three reasons: at launch we have no data, and BKT works with little data. We must explain the decision to a parent or a teacher, and BKT can be explained. And it gives us the data we would need later for a bigger model.
Say this

"A score is not enough. Right, right, wrong, wrong and wrong, wrong, right, right are both 50% — but they are opposite stories. One student is learning. One is getting lost."

"And a student can be right by luck, or wrong by a silly mistake."

"So instead of a score we keep a probability, and we update it after every answer. That is knowledge tracing."

"I would start with the simple method, Bayesian Knowledge Tracing. Not because it is the most accurate. Because at launch we have no data, and because we can explain it to a parent. A better model can come later."

Lesson 13How do we choose what the student studies next?

We recommend the topic that will teach them the most — not the one they will enjoy the most.

Think of it like…

Netflix wants you to keep watching. So it shows you what you like. That is the right goal for Netflix.

A gym coach does not give you the exercise you enjoy. They give you the one you need. You might not like it. You get stronger.

A learning platform is the coach, not Netflix. This is the most important difference, and many products get it wrong.

How the recommendation is built:

1  WHAT CAN THEY DO NOW?
   - which topics are they ready for? (need the earlier topics first)
   - which topics are weak?
   - which topics are due for review before they forget?

2  REMOVE what is not allowed
   - they are missing the earlier topic  → remove
   - wrong grade or wrong subject        → remove

3  ORDER the rest
   - which one will teach them the most?
   - is the difficulty right? (see below)
   - is there an exam coming?

4  SOMETIMES TRY SOMETHING NEW
   - if we always show the same thing, we never learn what works

5  EXPLAIN IT
   - "We suggest this because you missed two questions on fractions"
The right difficulty — think of it like…

Weights at the gym. Too light: no progress, and you are bored. Too heavy: you fail, and you stop coming.

The right weight is hard but possible. In learning, the usual target is a student getting about 70 to 85% correct. Hard enough to think. Easy enough to succeed.

Say this and they will notice: the most important part is not the algorithm. It is the map of the subject — which topic must come before which. Someone has to write down that you need basic algebra before quadratic equations. That is expert work, it is boring, it is invisible, and nothing works without it. Most candidates never mention it.
Say this

"Netflix recommends what you will enjoy. We recommend what will teach you the most. Often that is something the student would not choose."

"We pick topics they are ready for, remove the ones where they are missing the basics, and choose the right difficulty — hard enough to think, easy enough to succeed."

"And we explain why. 'We suggest this because you missed two questions on fractions last week.'"

"The hardest part is not the algorithm. It is the map of the subject — which topic comes before which. That is expert work and everything depends on it."

Lesson 14How would you build an AI tutor? The big question

Start with one rule: the tutor must not give the answer. Everything else comes from that.

Think of it like…

Your child is stuck on homework. Two parents:

Parent A says the answer. Homework is finished in 30 seconds. Everyone is happy. The child learned nothing.

Parent B asks: "What did you try? Show me. Ah — look at this step again. What happens if…?" Slower. Harder. The child can now do the next one alone.

We are building Parent B. If we build Parent A, we have built a very fast way to cheat.

The five steps of your answer:

1. Decide the behaviourAsk, don't tell. Find the mistake first. Give a small hint. Let the student finish.
2. Decide the inputsThe question + who this student is + which lesson they are in + our approved content.
3. Build itUnderstand the question → find the content (RAG) → add the student's level → write the answer → check it is safe and grounded.
4. Measure itIs the answer correct? Is it good teaching? And most of all: can the student now do it alone?
5. Launch smallOne subject. One grade. One language. Real people read the conversations every day in week 1.
The line that wins this question: "If our tutor solves the homework, we have built a cheating tool that looks very good in a demo." Say it exactly like that. It is short, it is memorable, and it shows you understand education — not just AI.
Say this

"I would not start with the model. I would start with what good tutoring is."

"The most important rule: the tutor must not give the answer. A good human tutor asks what you tried, finds the mistake, gives a hint, and lets you finish."

"If our tutor solves the homework, we have built a cheating tool that looks very good in a demo."

"Then it needs three things: the student's question, our approved content through RAG, and the learner model so it knows their level."

"And I would launch small. One subject, one grade. With real people reading the conversations every day in the first weeks."

5Part 5 — Measuring (the Product Lead part)

Lesson 15Why the normal metrics are dangerous here Key idea

In most products, more time spent = good. In education, more time spent can mean the student is lost.

Think of it like…

A driving school. School A: students pass after 20 hours. School B: students pass after 60 hours.

School B has 3× more "engagement". School B is worse.

Good teaching means less time for the same result, not more.

The dangerous pattern to watch for:

  • Students use the tutor a lot. ✅
  • They say they love it. ✅
  • They finish homework faster. ✅
  • They learn less. ❌

Why? Because the tutor became too helpful. It gives answers. Students feel great. They learned nothing. And you will not find out for a year.

Say this

"I would not use time on the platform as a success metric. In education more time can mean the student is lost, not engaged."

"There is a dangerous pattern: usage goes up, students love it, and learning goes down. That happens when the tutor becomes too helpful and gives answers."

"So the number I watch is: can the student do the next question without the tutor?"

Lesson 16What should we measure instead?

Three groups of numbers. Learn the groups, not every single metric.

GroupWhat it answersExamples
1. Learning
the reason we exist		 Did they actually learn? Test before and after · test again after 30 days · can they use it on a new type of problem · school results
2. Product
is it healthy		 Do they use it and stay? Did a new student have a real learning moment in session 1 · weekly active students · do they come back · do they finish · do they pay
3. The AI itself
is it working		 Is the AI good, fast and affordable? How often is it wrong · does the source really support the answer · how fast · cost per student per month · safety problems
The 30-day test is the important one. Almost anything improves the score today. Showing a student the full solution improves today's score and can reduce what they remember next month — because they never struggled. If you only measure at the end of the session, you will happily ship things that make students worse.

If they ask for ONE number (the "north star"):

Say this

"I would propose: topics learned and still remembered, per student, per week."

"'Learned' stops us rewarding activity with no learning. 'Still remembered' stops us rewarding cramming that disappears. 'Per student per week' keeps it honest."

"And one warning signal next to it: if that number goes up while students are dropping out, we are pushing too hard and losing the ones who need us most."

Lesson 17How do we prove a feature really helped?

Give it to some students and not to others. Then compare. That is an A/B test.

Think of it like…

Testing a medicine. You cannot give it to everybody and say "people got better." People get better anyway. You need a group who did not take it, to compare.

Same here. Students improve during the year anyway. Without a comparison group, you are taking credit for time passing.

The simple recipe:

1  Test students BEFORE          (what do they know now?)
2  Split them randomly into two groups
3  Group A gets the feature. Group B does not.
4  Wait — weeks, not days. Learning is slow.
5  Test both groups AFTER        (who improved more?)
6  Test AGAIN after 30 days      (who REMEMBERED more?)   ← the important one
7  Also check: did anyone get frustrated and stop?

Be honest about the hard parts. Interviewers like this:

  • Learning effects are small and slow. You need many students and several weeks.
  • If you only have a few hundred students, you may not be able to prove a small effect. Say so instead of pretending.
  • It feels wrong to hide a good feature from some children. The honest answer: we are testing exactly because we do not know it is good.
Say this

"I would test students before, split them randomly, give the feature to one group only, and test both groups after."

"And then test again 30 days later. That one matters most. Almost anything improves the score today. Showing the full solution improves today's score and can reduce what they remember next month, because they never had to struggle."

"I would also be honest that this needs weeks and a lot of students. If we are too small to prove it, I would say that instead of reading noise."

6Part 6 — Safety and student data

Lesson 18Protecting children's data

The users are children. So the rule is not "follow the law". The rule is collect as little as possible.

Think of it like…

The safest way to protect money is not a better safe. It is to not keep cash in the building.

Same with data. Data you never collected cannot leak, cannot be stolen, and cannot be misused.

Five things to say:

1. Collect lessFor every field, ask: what breaks if we don't have it? We need their answers and progress. We rarely need their photo or exact address.
2. Parents must agreeFor young children, a parent has to give permission. The rules differ by country. I would take legal advice and build for the strictest one.
3. Careful with outside AI companiesIf student conversations go to an outside model, we need a written promise: they do not keep it and do not train on it. And remove names before sending.
4. Never train on student dataNot without clear, separate permission. Most parents would say no — and they would be right.
5. Control who sees whatA teacher sees their class. Not other classes. And we record every access.
Use your own experience here. You built exactly this in HR Insight AI: four roles, access controlled in the database itself, and a log of every action. Same problem — sensitive data about real people. Mention it.
Say this

"They are children, so I start by collecting as little as possible. Data we never collect cannot leak."

"The model does not need the child's name to explain fractions. So we remove names before sending anything to an outside AI company."

"And we never train on student data without clear permission."

"I built this pattern before, in my attrition project — four roles, access controlled at the database level, and every action logged."

Lesson 19What if the AI teaches something wrong?

It will happen. The question is whether you have a plan.

Five steps:

1. Find itDo not wait for students — they cannot see the mistake. Use automatic checks, an easy report button, and teachers.
2. Stop itIs it one bad answer, or a mistake in our content that everyone received? Turn off that topic if needed.
3. Fix the students
this step is special		Students learned the wrong thing. Find who received it. Tell them. Give the correct version. Make them practise again.
4. Find the causeBad content? Wrong content found? Model invented it? Nothing to find and it guessed? Different fixes.
5. Tell the teachersA school that discovers it alone will leave. A school you told will often trust you more.
Step 3 is your best point. In normal software you fix the bug and move on. Here, the damage is inside the student's head. You have to go back and correct it. Almost nobody says this in an interview.
Say this

"In normal software, you fix the bug and move on. Here you cannot. The student already learned the wrong thing."

"So there is an extra step: find the students who saw it, send them the correction, and make them practise again. We need the logging to make that possible before we ever need it."

"And tell the teachers ourselves. A school that finds it alone will not renew."

7Part 7 — Arabic (your strongest topic)

Lesson 20Why Arabic is hard for AI — and why you win here

You are a native Arabic speaker from Tunisia. Almost nobody else applying can answer this. Steer the conversation here.

Problem 1 — people write one language and speak another.

Think of it like…

The textbook and the exam are in Modern Standard Arabic (MSA). But nobody speaks MSA at home. It is nobody's first language.

At home, students speak a dialect: Egyptian, Gulf, Levantine, or Maghrebi. These are different enough that speakers sometimes do not understand each other.

You are Tunisian. You speak Derja — the dialect that is furthest from MSA, and the one AI handles worst. Say this. It is real, it is specific, and it is memorable.

The product problem: the student asks in dialect, but must be taught in MSA — because the exam is in MSA.

Problem 2 — Arabic costs more money.

Arabic uses about 2 to 3 times more tokens per word than English. Remember: we pay per token. So the same lesson costs more and is slower. And less fits on the "desk".

Problem 3 — other technical issues.

No short vowelsArabic is normally written without the small marks. So one written word can mean different things.
Right to leftArabic goes right to left, but maths and code go left to right. Mixed together, it breaks on screen. Real problem for a science product.
Mixed writingStudents write Arabizi — Arabic using Latin letters and numbers. In the Maghreb they mix in French too.
No good testsThere are very few Arabic tests to measure AI quality. Almost none for dialects. We must build our own.
Say this — your differentiator

"Arabic is not a translation problem. It is a different kind of problem."

"Students speak a dialect at home, but the textbook and the exam are in Modern Standard Arabic. So the student asks in dialect and must be taught in MSA."

"I am Tunisian. I speak Derja, which is the furthest from MSA and the one models handle worst. So I have a good feeling for where this breaks."

"Two practical points. Arabic uses two to three times more tokens per word than English, so our cost per student is higher than an English test suggests. And English quality tests tell us nothing here — we would have to build our own with teachers who speak each dialect."

If they ask "so which language should the tutor answer in?"

"Accept everything on the way in — dialect, MSA, Arabizi, mixed French. A student who writes the way they speak must not be told the system does not understand."

"Answer mainly in MSA, because the exam is in MSA. But explain the idea in simple language first, then give them the MSA word they will be tested on. Teach the word and the idea."

"And I would test that rather than assume it. It is a teaching question, not a technical one."

8Part 8 — How to answer, in the room

Lesson 21The formula for any technical question

Four steps. Always the same four. If you forget everything else, use this.

1  WHAT PROBLEM does it solve?        "We need X because…"
2  HOW does it work, simply?          "First… then… then…"
3  WHEN would I use it — and NOT?     "I'd use it for X. Not for Y, because…"
4  HOW do I MEASURE it?               "I'd know it works if…"

Example — "What is RAG?" answered with the formula:

Watch the four steps

(1 Problem) "The model answers from memory, so it can be wrong and we cannot show the source."

(2 How) "So first we find the right lesson from our own content. Then we give it to the model with the question. It answers using that."

(3 When) "I use it for anything factual — the curriculum. I would not use it for teaching style; that is fine-tuning."

(4 Measure) "I would measure two things separately: did we find the right content, and did the answer really come from it. If you only measure the final answer you cannot tell which part failed."

Step 4 is what makes you sound like a Product Manager. Engineers usually stop at step 2. Never finish an answer without saying how you would measure it.
Lesson 22English survival phrases for the interview

English is your third language. Use these. They make you sound calm and professional — not unsure.

When you did not understand the question

"Could you say that another way, please?"

"Just to make sure I understood — do you mean X, or Y?"

"Can you give me an example of what you mean?"

When you need a few seconds to think

"That's a good question. Let me think for a moment."

"Let me structure my answer. There are three parts to this."

Silence for 3 seconds is completely normal. It looks thoughtful, not slow.

When you do not know

"I don't know that one. Here is how I would find out."

"I haven't worked with that directly. What I have done that is close is…"

This is a strong answer, not a weak one. Inventing an answer is the weak one — and an interviewer will always notice.

When you want to check you answered the question

"Does that answer your question, or would you like me to go deeper?"

"Was that the level of detail you wanted?"

When you lose your sentence in the middle

"Sorry — let me say that again more clearly."

Do not apologise twice. Say it once, then continue. Everyone does this, including native speakers.

One real rule: speak slower than feels natural. When we are nervous in a second or third language, we speed up, and that is when words disappear. Slow speech sounds confident. It also gives your brain time to find the next word.
