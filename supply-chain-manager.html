Supply Chain Manager

Four years and nine months owning end-to-end fulfillment for third-party sellers at Jumia — inbound, quality control, inventory, dispatch, last-mile and the entire reverse chain. This is your deepest experience. The one thing you must defend: why you're coming to this from an engineering seat.

33 questions Every answer scripted Full mock transcript

Your positioning for this role — decide this before you walk in

You are not a developer trying supply chain, and you are not a warehouse manager who wandered into tech. You are a supply chain manager who can build the systems the supply chain runs on. Almost nobody in this hiring pool can say that. Every answer in this file is engineered to land that one sentence from a different angle.

The trap to avoid: spending interview time on your developer job. Mention it deliberately, as evidence — "I now build the reporting platforms operations teams use" — then steer straight back to the warehouse floor. If half the interview is about NestJS, you have lost the role.

1Round 1 — The recruiter screen (20–30 minutes)

This round is not about competence. It's a filter: can you speak clearly, do your dates make sense, are you affordable, and is the career switch a problem. Keep every answer under 90 seconds.

1. Tell me about yourself.
Say this

"I've spent seven years at Jumia, Africa's largest online marketplace, and the core of that is nearly five years as Supply Chain Manager running fulfillment for our third-party sellers."

"Concretely, that meant I owned the chain end to end for sellers who stored their stock in our warehouse — around [X] sellers and [Y] orders a day. Inbound receiving and put-away, quality inspection on arrival, inventory control, picking and packing, dispatch planning, handing over to last-mile carriers, and then the entire reverse chain: returns, failed deliveries, inspection and grading, restock-or-reject, and seller reimbursement. I led a team of [N] and planned their shifts against daily order volume."

"The thread through all of it is that I ran the operation on data. I defined the KPI set we measured ourselves against — dispatch SLA, picking accuracy, inventory accuracy, defect rate, return rate, failed-delivery rate — and I built the Power BI and Excel reporting management reviewed. That's how I found the two sellers driving a disproportionate share of our defects and returns, and it's how I got moved onto central-team projects designing control processes rolled out beyond my own site."

"Since 2024 I've been on Jumia's central technology team building the internal reporting platforms operations teams run on — which means I now know both sides of that system. And that's exactly why this role interests me: it's a supply chain job where being able to get at the data yourself is an advantage rather than a nice-to-have."

Why this works: it is chronological, it opens with tenure and scale, it lists concrete process steps that prove you actually did the job (a fake would say "managed logistics"), and it lands the tech move at the end as an asset rather than a detour — pre-empting their biggest doubt before they raise it. Roughly 75 seconds spoken.
Don't: start with "So, I graduated from Carthage University in 2018..." Nobody wants your life story chronologically from birth. Start at the thing they're hiring for.
2. You're a software developer right now. Why are you applying for a supply chain role? Make-or-break

This is the question that decides the interview. They are worried you'll be bored in six months and leave for a developer salary. Your answer must be positive and forward-looking — never framed as retreat, never as "tech didn't work out."

Say this

"It's a fair question and I want to answer it directly. I didn't leave supply chain because I disliked it — I loved it. I left because I kept hitting a specific wall: I could see a problem clearly in the data and I couldn't fix the system causing it. I'd identify that our returns were spiking on a category because of inaccurate listings, and then I'd raise a ticket and wait weeks. So I went and learned to build the systems myself."

"Two and a half years on the engineering side taught me exactly what I went to learn: how the data model behind an operation actually works, how to get an answer out of a database myself instead of requesting a report, and what's genuinely quick to change versus what people just say is quick."

"What I've realised is that I don't want to be a developer building tools for other people's operations. I want to run the operation — and be the manager who doesn't have to wait in a queue to understand it. Supply chain is becoming a data discipline; the managers who'll be effective in five years are the ones who can interrogate the system themselves. That's the job I've spent seven years accidentally training for, and it's this one."

Expect these follow-ups — have the answers ready:
  • "So you'd take a pay cut?" → "I've looked at the market for this level and the range you've advertised works for me. I'm optimising for owning an operation, not for a title."
  • "What stops you going back to tech in a year?" → "Nothing stops anyone doing anything. What I'd say is I already made that move deliberately and I know what's on the other side of it — that's precisely why I'm confident about this one. I'm not guessing."
  • "Won't you be frustrated without the technical work?" → "I'll still be doing it — just pointed at the operation. Building the analysis behind a seller scorecard is the technical work I enjoy most, and it directly moves the KPIs."
Never say: "I wasn't happy as a developer", "the tech industry is tough right now", "I missed working with people." All three say I'm running from something. You are running toward something.
3. Why are you leaving Jumia after seven years?
Say this

"Seven years at one company was a deliberate choice — I had two very different careers inside it and I was still learning in both. But I've now done the internal move I wanted to do, and the next step I'm after isn't available to me there: I want to come back to operations with the technical skill set attached, in a role where I own the whole chain and the improvement agenda."

"I'd also say honestly that after seven years in one environment I want to see how a different organisation solves these problems. Jumia taught me e-commerce logistics in a genuinely hard market — [mention a real constraint: address quality, cash-on-delivery, carrier reliability]. I want to take that somewhere new rather than keep solving the same version of it."

Why this works: long tenure is an asset — frame it as loyalty plus range, not as being stuck. "The next step isn't available there" is the cleanest possible reason to leave: it's about growth, it criticises nobody, and it can't be checked.
Don't criticise Jumia. Not the pay, not the management, not the restructuring. Interviewers hear one thing: this is how they'll talk about us.
4. Walk me through your CV. There's a gap in the logic — you were a manager, then a developer.
Say this

"Let me make it simple. April 2019 to December 2023, Supply Chain Manager at Jumia. From January 2024 to today, software engineer on Jumia's central team. Same company throughout, no gaps."

"The overlap is the part worth knowing: from April 2022 to April 2023 I did a full-stack development diploma at 3W Academy, evenings and weekends, while running the warehouse operation full time. I also did a short front-end internship in late 2023 to prove I could ship. Then Jumia moved me internally."

"So it isn't a gap or a break — it's twelve months of doing two full-time things at once because I'd decided I wanted the skill. If you want a data point on how I work, that's probably the best one on the page."

Why this works: it reframes the weird part of your CV as the proof of your work ethic. Anyone who has done a demanding evening course while managing a warehouse knows exactly what that costs, and it reads as drive rather than instability.
5. What are your salary expectations?

Rule: never name the first number if you can avoid it. Two deflections, then commit to a range.

First attempt — deflect

"I'd rather understand the scope properly first — the team size, the volume, whether the reverse chain is in scope — because those change the level significantly. Do you have a band budgeted for the role?"

If they insist — commit to a range

"Based on what I've seen for supply chain management roles at this scope, I'm looking at [X] to [Y] per month gross. I'd weight that toward the top end given I bring the analytics and systems side as well, which most candidates for this role won't. But I'm more interested in the scope and the team than in optimising the last few percent — is that in line with your band?"

If they ask your current salary: "My current package is in an engineering band, so it isn't really a comparable benchmark for this role — I'd rather we talk about the market rate for this scope. What's the range you've budgeted?" In many jurisdictions they can't require the answer; deflecting politely is always acceptable.
6. What do you know about us, and why this company?
Say this — fill the brackets with real research

"Three things pulled me toward you. First, [a specific operational fact: your own fulfillment network / you've just opened a second warehouse / you run your own last-mile fleet] — that's the kind of chain I want to own, because the interesting problems are where you control the handover points."

"Second, [something about growth or the market: expanding into X, doubling SKU count, moving from 3PL to in-house]. Growth is where supply chain management is actually hard — holding SLA while volume moves is a different job from maintaining a steady state, and it's the one I've done."

"Third, honestly, the job description read like it was written for the way I work — it mentions [KPI ownership / continuous improvement / data], and that's the part of the role I'd be doing whether or not anyone asked me to."

Don't: recite their About page. "You're a leader in your sector and I admire your values" tells them you did no research. One specific operational fact beats three paragraphs of mission statement.

2Round 2 — The operation (hiring manager, 45–60 minutes)

This is the real interview. A supply chain manager interviewing you can tell within three questions whether you actually ran an operation or just supervised one. Answer with process steps, decision rules and exception handling — that's the vocabulary of someone who was really there.

7. Describe the operation you ran. Give me the scale.
Say this

"Jumia's marketplace model means third-party sellers can store inventory in our warehouse and we fulfill on their behalf. I owned that program operationally — so the seller's stock became my responsibility from the moment it arrived at the dock to the moment it was delivered, and again if it came back."

"Scale: roughly [Y] orders a day, across [X] active sellers and [Z] SKUs, with a team of [N] across [number] shifts. Volume was heavily seasonal — Black Friday and our anniversary campaign could take us to [multiple]× a normal day, which is really what tested the process."

"My scope covered inbound receiving and put-away, quality inspection, inventory accuracy, picking, packing, dispatch planning, carrier handover, in-transit exception follow-up, and the whole reverse chain. Plus seller relationship management on the operational side — onboarding them into fulfillment, scorecards, escalations, and recommending offboarding when a seller was costing us more than they brought in."

Likely follow-up — "What was your peak day?" Have a number and a story: "Black Friday [year], we did [N] orders against a normal [M]. We held SLA because I'd pre-picked the top [N] SKUs into a forward-pick zone the week before and staged packing materials at each station rather than centrally."
8. Walk me through your inbound receiving process, step by step.
Say this

"Seller books an inbound appointment against an expected stock declaration — SKU, quantity, packaging format. That booking matters, because unannounced arrivals are what wreck a receiving dock."

"On arrival we check the delivery against the declaration: right seller, right SKUs, right quantities. Then quality inspection — I'll come back to that in detail because it was the part I tightened most. Anything conforming gets counted in, labelled to our barcode standard, and put away to a location the system assigns; anything non-conforming goes to a quarantine area, never onto the shelf."

"Then reconciliation: system quantity against physical quantity, and any discrepancy is documented against the seller before we accept the stock. That's the step people skip when they're busy, and it's exactly the step that produces an inventory discrepancy three weeks later that nobody can explain."

"The rule I enforced was simple: stock is either received clean, or it's quarantined — it never goes to a pick face in an unknown state. The cost of a defective unit discovered at inbound is a few minutes of handling. The cost of discovering it at the customer's door is the delivery, the return leg, the refund, the review, and the customer."

Why this works: the closing line is the single most quotable thing you can say in a fulfillment interview. It shows you think in total cost rather than in warehouse-local cost, which is the difference between a supervisor and a manager.
9. How did you run quality control on inbound seller stock?
Say this

"Four checks, in order. Conformity — is the physical product actually the product on the listing: right model, right colour, right size, right quantity per pack. Damage — the unit itself and its packaging, because a scuffed retail box gets refused at the door even if the product inside is fine. Labelling — barcode present, scannable, matching our SKU. And packaging adequacy — will this survive the last mile, which in our market is a real question."

"On sampling: I didn't inspect everything at 100%, that doesn't scale. I ran a risk-based approach — higher inspection rates for new sellers, for categories with a history of defects [e.g. fashion sizing, electronics accessories], and for any seller whose scorecard had moved the wrong way. Established sellers with a clean record got sampled. That way inspection effort went where the defects actually were."

"Non-conformance had a fixed path: quarantine the units, photograph the defect, log it against the seller with a reason code, and then either reject the consignment back to the seller or accept with a documented deduction. The reason code is the important part — it turns a one-off complaint into a dataset. After a few months I could show a seller 'this is your defect rate, this is the category average, here are the three codes driving your number,' and that conversation lands very differently from 'your quality is poor.'"

Follow-up — "Give me an example of a defect pattern you found." "[Fill with a real one.] The pattern I saw most was listing inaccuracy rather than physical damage — the product was fine, but the listing described something slightly different, so the customer refused at delivery. That shows up as a delivery failure, not as a quality defect, which is why it went unnoticed until I coded returns by root cause and the two datasets connected."
10. How did you maintain inventory accuracy?
Say this

"Three layers. Prevention at the transaction points — every movement scanned, because accuracy is lost at receiving, put-away and picking, not mysteriously overnight. Detection through cycle counting rather than waiting for an annual full count. And correction with root cause, so the same discrepancy doesn't recur."

"On cycle counting I prioritised by value and velocity — fast-moving and high-value SKUs counted far more often than slow-moving long-tail items, because that's where a discrepancy actually costs you an order. I also triggered a count off events rather than only off the schedule: any pick that came up short triggered an immediate count of that location, since a short pick is the operation telling you your record is wrong right now."

"The correction step is where most people stop too early. Adjusting the system to match the shelf makes the number look right and guarantees you'll do it again next month. So every material discrepancy got a cause — mis-put-away, mis-pick, receiving error, damage not recorded, theft — and I tracked the causes as a distribution. When one cause dominated, that was a process or a training problem, and I fixed it there rather than by counting more often."

Follow-up — "What accuracy did you hold?" Give the number if you have it ([%]) and the denominator you measured it on — by location, by SKU, or by unit. If you don't remember, say so and describe the measure: "I measured it at location level, which is the stricter measure — a SKU can reconcile overall while two locations are both wrong."
11. Talk me through a normal day — how did you plan dispatch?
Say this

"I worked backwards from carrier cut-off, not forwards from order arrival. That's the whole discipline of dispatch planning. If the carrier collects at [time], then packing has to be finished by [time], so picking has to be complete by [time], so I need to know by mid-morning whether my order volume fits my labour."

"Morning: look at the overnight order bank against yesterday's actuals and the same day last week, and compare that to who's actually on shift. If volume is above what the shift can absorb, I'm making a decision at 9am, not at 4pm when it's too late. The levers were moving people from put-away onto picking — put-away can absorb a few hours of delay, dispatch can't — bringing in overtime, or, as a last resort, prioritising by promise date so anything expedited or at risk of breaching SLA went first."

"Then I managed exceptions through the day: short picks, stock not found at location, items failing the packing check. Those are the things that silently eat the buffer. And after handover I tracked what the carrier actually collected against what we staged, because 'dispatched' in our system and 'on the van' are not the same thing, and the gap between them is somebody's missed delivery tomorrow."

Why this works: "I work backwards from carrier cut-off" and "dispatched in the system isn't on the van" are both things only someone who has actually run a dispatch operation says. They are worth more than any KPI you could quote.
12. How did you manage last-mile performance and carriers?
Say this

"I tracked delivery performance as my own metric even though the leg wasn't physically mine — because the customer doesn't distinguish, and a failed delivery lands back in my warehouse as a return either way."

"The metric that mattered most was failed-delivery rate, segmented by cause. I split it into three buckets: address quality — incomplete or wrong addresses, which is a real issue in our market; customer unavailability; and refusal at the door. Those have completely different owners. Address problems are a data-capture fix upstream at checkout. Unavailability is a communication fix — contact before the attempt, better delivery windows. Refusal is almost always a product or listing problem, which pointed straight back at my seller scorecards."

"Segmenting it that way is what made it actionable. Before that, 'failed delivery rate is X' was just a number everyone complained about in a meeting and nobody owned. Once I could show [N]% of failures were refusals concentrated in [category], it stopped being a last-mile problem and became a listings problem — and it got fixed."

"On the carrier side, weekly performance review against SLA, and I pushed hard on reducing repeat attempts — every re-attempt is cost with a falling probability of success. Past a certain number of attempts you're better off recovering the order a different way than sending the van a third time."

Follow-up — "How would you improve on-time delivery with a carrier you can't replace?" "Fix what's on my side of the handover first: dispatch earlier in their window rather than at the cut-off, improve address data quality before the parcel ships, and reduce the parcels that were always going to fail. Then give them clean segmented data in the weekly review — carriers respond much better to 'these 40 parcels failed for this specific reason in this zone' than to 'your SLA is down.'"
13. Tell me about your reverse logistics process. Your differentiator

Lead with this if given the chance. Most fulfillment candidates have almost nothing to say about returns. You owned the full chain.

Say this

"I owned the complete reverse chain, and it covered two different flows that people often conflate: customer returns after a successful delivery, and failed or undelivered orders that never reached the customer. They arrive at the same dock but they're different problems — one is a product or expectation failure, the other is a delivery or data failure."

"Process: reception and matching back to the original order — an unmatched return is a unit you've lost twice. Then inspection and grading: is it sellable as new, sellable with repackaging, or not sellable. That grading decision is where the money is, because it decides whether the unit re-enters inventory at full value or is written off."

"Then the restock-or-reject decision, the seller reimbursement where the fault sat with the seller, and — the part I'd argue is most important — root-cause coding every single return. Not a free-text note. A fixed code list, applied consistently, so that returns become a dataset rather than a pile of anecdotes."

"That coding is what turned returns from a cost centre into my best diagnostic. Once I had a few months of it in Power BI, I could see which causes, which categories and which sellers actually drove volume — and it was heavily concentrated. A small number of sellers and a couple of causes accounted for a disproportionate share. That's an actionable finding. 'Return rate is [X]%' isn't."

Follow-up — "What did you do with that finding?" "Three things. Corrected the listings where the product didn't match the description, because that was the biggest single cause. Tightened packaging requirements for the sellers whose units were arriving damaged. And escalated — then recommended offboarding — the sellers whose defect and return numbers stayed bad after we'd given them the data and a window to fix it. Offboarding a seller is a commercial decision, not mine to make alone, but I made sure the commercial team made it with the actual numbers in front of them."
14. How did you manage third-party seller performance?
Say this

"Scorecards, and the discipline to actually use them. Each seller was measured on the things they controlled: inbound quality — defect and rejection rate at receiving — packaging and labelling compliance, listing accuracy as evidenced by refusals and returns, and their return rate against the category average. Category average matters; a 12% return rate is alarming in electronics and normal in fashion, so an absolute threshold punishes the wrong people."

"The escalation path was deliberately boring and predictable: share the data, agree a corrective action with a deadline, re-measure, and if it hasn't moved, escalate commercially with the evidence attached. No surprises. A seller who's been shown their numbers monthly can't claim they were blindsided when the conversation gets serious."

"The reason I insisted on running it that way is that 'this seller is a problem' is an opinion and it gets argued with — usually by whoever owns the commercial relationship. 'This seller's defect rate is [X] against a category average of [Y], it's been above target for four months, and they're generating [Z]% of our returns on [W]% of our volume' is a fact. Facts end arguments much faster."

Why this works: it shows commercial awareness — you understand that offboarding a seller costs revenue and isn't purely an ops call — while still demonstrating you held the line. That balance is exactly what a hiring manager is listening for in a manager-level candidate.
15. Which KPIs did you own? What were your targets?
Say this

"I defined the operational KPI set and built the reporting behind it, which meant I owned both the numbers and the definitions. Seven core metrics."

"Dispatch SLA — percentage of orders dispatched within the promised window. That was the headline number, target [X]%. Picking accuracy — right item, right quantity, first time, because a mis-pick costs you the return leg and the customer. Inventory accuracy at location level. Defect rate at inbound inspection. Return rate, tracked by category and by seller rather than as one blended number. Failed-delivery rate, segmented by cause. And order cycle time — order placed to delivered — as the customer-facing end-to-end measure."

"I built those into Power BI reports and Excel Power Query dashboards that management reviewed, with one view for the team on the floor and one for management. Those are different products: the team needs today against target, right now, in a form you can read across a room. Management needs the trend and the outliers. I learned that the hard way after building one dashboard for both audiences and watching nobody use it."

Follow-up — "Which one would you protect if you could only hold one?" A great question to have a crisp answer for: "Dispatch SLA — because it's the one my team fully controls and it's upstream of everything else. Cycle time depends on the carrier, return rate depends on the seller. But if I'm being honest about what matters to the business rather than to my scorecard, it's order cycle time, because that's what the customer experiences and what brings them back."
16. What systems did you work in?
Say this

"Day to day, [the real WMS/OMS name] plus Jumia's internal fulfillment tooling — that's where receiving, put-away, picking and dispatch all lived. Power BI and Excel with Power Query for all the reporting and analysis. Jira and Confluence for process documentation and for raising system issues."

"I'd add something most candidates can't: because I now work on the engineering side, I understand what's actually happening underneath those systems. I write SQL daily and I do relational data modeling, so when a report disagrees with the floor I can usually work out whether it's an operational problem or a data problem. That distinction used to cost my team days."

"And on a new system — I've learned two stacks from scratch in the last four years. Whatever you run, I'll be productive in it quickly. What doesn't change is the process logic underneath it."

Don't leave the WMS blank. If your operation ran on an internal tool with no public name, say so explicitly — "Jumia's internal WMS, built in-house" — rather than saying "a WMS". Vagueness here reads as bluffing.
17. You mention working with a central team on new control processes. Tell me about that.
Say this

"That came out of the analysis rather than out of a mandate. Because I was root-cause-coding defects and returns consistently, I had evidence that other sites didn't — and when the central team looked at why my numbers were moving, the answer was the control process I'd put around intake and inspection."

"So I was brought in to help design and roll out standards beyond my own site: risk-based inspection rather than blanket inspection, a consistent non-conformance reason code list, and standardised intake and returns workflows so the data was comparable between sites. That last part sounds administrative and it's the whole point — if two warehouses code the same defect differently, you can't compare them, and you can't find the systemic problem."

"What I took from it: rolling a process out is a completely different skill from designing one. The design took a few weeks. Getting it adopted took months, and it only worked where I got the site leads to see their own numbers first. Nobody adopts your SOP because it's well written. They adopt it when they've seen what it will do for a number they're already being measured on."

Why this works: this is your strongest seniority signal in the whole interview. Influence beyond your own scope, without formal authority over the people you needed, is precisely the step from supervisor to manager.

3Round 3 — Behavioral (STAR)

Every answer below is structured Situation → Task → Action → Result. Keep Situation to two sentences; interviewers score the Action. Say "I", not "we".

18. Tell me about the worst operational crisis you've handled. STAR
Say this — swap in your real incident

Situation. "During [peak campaign / Black Friday year], volume went to roughly [N]× a normal day and we were missing dispatch cut-off by mid- afternoon on day two. Orders were ageing in the bank, and every order that misses cut-off doesn't just arrive late — it collides with the next day's volume."

Task. "I had to protect dispatch SLA without more people than I had, and stop the backlog compounding into day three."

Action. "I did four things. First I stopped put-away and moved those people onto picking — put-away can be deferred for hours, dispatch can't, and I made that trade explicitly knowing I'd pay for it in location accuracy later. Second, I prioritised the order bank by promise date rather than by order sequence, so anything about to breach went first instead of first-in-first-out. Third, I pre-staged packing materials at each station instead of a central supply point, because I'd watched packers spend real minutes walking. Fourth, I set up a mid-shift checkpoint against a target line instead of finding out at the end of the day — if we were behind at the checkpoint, we escalated to overtime immediately."

Result. "We cleared the backlog by [end of day three] and held dispatch SLA at [X]% across the peak. Afterwards I made two of those changes permanent: the mid-shift checkpoint, and pre-staging materials — that one cost nothing and I should have done it a year earlier. The put-away deferral I wrote up as a peak-only measure with a mandatory cycle count afterwards, because it does cost you accuracy and I wanted that trade to be a conscious decision next time rather than a panic."

Why this works: naming the cost of your own decision — "I made that trade explicitly knowing I'd pay for it in location accuracy" — is the single most senior-sounding thing in this file. Junior candidates describe decisions as free. Managers describe trade-offs.
19. Tell me about a time your analysis changed a decision. STAR
Say this

Situation. "Our return rate was being discussed as a warehouse quality problem — the assumption was that we were shipping damaged goods, and the proposed fix was more inspection at outbound."

Task. "I wasn't convinced, because our outbound packing checks weren't finding much. I wanted to know what was actually causing returns before we spent labour on a control that might not touch the cause."

Action. "I'd already introduced root-cause coding on every return, so I had a few months of consistent data. I built the analysis in Power BI cut three ways: by cause code, by category, and by seller. The picture was completely different from the assumption. Damage in transit was a minority of volume. The dominant cause was the product not matching the listing — customers receiving something that wasn't what they thought they'd ordered and refusing it or sending it back. And it was concentrated: a small group of sellers were producing a share of returns far out of proportion to their share of orders."

Result. "That killed the outbound inspection proposal, which would have added cost to every single order to address a minority cause. Instead we corrected the listings for the worst offenders, tightened packaging requirements where transit damage was real, and put the concentrated sellers on a formal corrective plan with offboarding as the endpoint. Return rate moved [by X / in the right direction], and more importantly we stopped spending effort on the wrong control. The lasting change was that returns stopped being an anecdote in a meeting and became a monthly number with named causes."

Why this works: you didn't just produce an analysis — you prevented a bad decision. That's a stronger story than an improvement, because it shows judgement rather than effort. It also quietly demonstrates you can disagree with a prevailing view and win with evidence.
20. Tell me about a difficult conflict with a supplier, seller or stakeholder. STAR
Say this

Situation. "I had a high-volume seller whose defect rate at inbound was consistently well above their category average. Commercially they mattered — real revenue — so there was genuine pressure to keep accepting their stock and absorb the problem."

Task. "I needed the quality fixed without simply blocking a seller the business depended on, which wasn't my call to make unilaterally anyway."

Action. "I stopped arguing about it and built the case. I pulled their defect rate against the category average, their share of our returns against their share of orders, and the handling cost of the rejections and re-work their consignments generated — so the conversation moved from 'quality is bad' to 'this is what it costs us.' Then I sat down with them and the commercial owner together, showed the same numbers to both, and proposed a specific corrective plan: packaging changes on the two SKUs driving most of it, and a temporary 100% inspection on their inbound until the rate came down, with a defined review date."

Result. "They accepted it, largely because the data made it hard to argue with and because I gave them a route back rather than just a penalty. Their defect rate came down over [period] and we kept the revenue. What I learned is that presenting a problem with the commercial cost attached, and offering a defined path out, gets you a completely different response from escalating it as a compliance issue. I also learned to bring the commercial owner into the room rather than fighting them in email afterwards."

21. Tell me about a time you led a change people resisted. STAR
Say this

Situation. "When I introduced mandatory root-cause coding on returns, the team pushed back hard — and fairly. It was extra clicks on every single unit, at the end of a shift, and from where they stood it was paperwork that helped somebody else."

Task. "I needed it applied consistently, because inconsistently coded data is worse than no data — it looks like a finding and it isn't."

Action. "I made three changes. I cut the code list down — my first version had far too many options, which is how you get everything coded 'other'. I made the codes match the words the team actually used, not warehouse-management vocabulary. And then I did the thing that actually worked: a month in, I brought the first report back to the team and showed them what their own data had found — that a specific seller was generating a large share of their re-work. They could see their effort producing a consequence for someone else rather than for them."

Result. "Compliance went from patchy to consistent within about [period], and it became the dataset behind the analysis that redirected our whole returns strategy. The lesson I took is that resistance to a new process is almost never laziness — it's a reasonable response to being asked for effort with no visible return. Showing people what their data did is the cheapest change management there is."

22. How do you manage and develop a team? Tell me about a poor performer. STAR
Say this

"My management style is specific expectations, visible numbers, and no surprises. Everyone on my team knew what the target was and where we stood against it daily — I put it up where they could see it, because a team that can't see the score can't play."

Situation. "I had a picker whose accuracy was consistently below the rest of the team. The easy read was carelessness."

Task. "Get the accuracy up, or establish that it wasn't going to move."

Action. "I looked at the errors before I spoke to him, and they weren't random — they clustered on a specific set of SKUs, visually similar variants where the difference was a size or a shade. That's not carelessness, that's a process exposing someone to an error trap. So I did two things: worked with him on a scan-verify habit specifically at those locations, and separated the confusable SKUs physically so they weren't adjacent on the pick face. I also set a weekly check-in with his own number so he could see progress rather than only hearing from me when something went wrong."

Result. "His accuracy came up to team level within [period], and the SKU separation reduced errors for everyone, not just him. The general lesson is the one I try to apply every time: look at the pattern in someone's errors before you conclude it's about the person. Most of the time you're looking at a process defect wearing a person's name."

Follow-up — "And if it had been about the person?" Have this ready: "Then it's a documented conversation with a clear standard and a timeframe, support to get there, and a decision at the end of it. I'd rather have that conversation early and directly than let someone fail slowly — that's worse for them than it is for me."
23. Tell me about a mistake you made that cost the business. They're testing honesty

You must have a real answer. "I can't think of one" reads as either dishonest or unself-aware — both fatal at manager level. Pick something real, mid-sized, fixed, and learned from.

Say this

Situation. "Early on, I rolled out a revised intake process across the team based on what I'd seen working on one shift. I was confident in it, I documented it, I trained it, and I rolled it out to everyone at once."

Task. "I wanted faster, more consistent receiving."

Action / what went wrong. "It didn't hold. The shift I'd designed it on had a stock profile that didn't match the others — different mix, different arrival patterns — and a step that was trivial there was a bottleneck elsewhere. Within two weeks people had quietly reverted to the old way, which is worse than never changing it, because now half the team is doing one thing and half the other and your data means nothing."

Result. "I pulled it back, ran it as a pilot on one other shift first, adjusted the step that broke, and re-rolled it properly. It worked the second time. What I actually changed permanently was my own default: I never roll a process out to everyone off a single sample again. Pilot, adjust, then scale. That habit is a large part of why the later central-team rollout went as well as it did — I'd already paid for that lesson."

Why this works: it's a genuine failure with a real business consequence, it's a manager-level mistake rather than a trivial one, and the lesson visibly transfers into a later success you've already mentioned. That last connection is what makes it land.
24. Tell me about a time you influenced people you had no authority over. STAR
Say this

Situation. "The failed-delivery analysis I'd done pointed at two things outside my control: address data captured at checkout, and listing accuracy owned by the commercial and content teams. Neither reported to me, and both had their own priorities that had nothing to do with my warehouse."

Task. "Get changes made in two other teams' backlogs without any authority to ask for them."

Action. "I stopped presenting it as my problem. For the commercial team I translated it into their currency — this many refused deliveries, concentrated in these listings, is this much lost revenue and this much re-handling. For the last-mile team I gave them something genuinely useful rather than a complaint: their failures segmented by cause and by zone, which they didn't have, so my analysis made their number easier to move too. And I asked for something small and specific from each rather than a programme of work."

Result. "Both moved. The listings got corrected for the worst offenders, and last mile changed their contact-before-attempt approach in the zones where unavailability dominated. The principle I took away: you don't get cross-functional change by being right. You get it by doing the translation work yourself, so that acting on your finding is the easiest thing on someone else's list."

4Round 4 — Case questions

For cases, they're scoring your structure, not your answer. Always: clarify → frame the drivers → prioritise → say what you'd do first → name how you'd measure it. Think out loud. Ask at least one clarifying question before you start — candidates who dive straight in score worse.

25. Peak is three weeks away. Dispatch SLA is at 82% against a 95% target. What do you do?
Open by clarifying

"Before I answer — two questions. Has it been at 82% for a while, or has it dropped recently? A chronic 82% and a sudden drop are completely different problems. And do I know whether the misses are spread across all orders or concentrated in a segment — a category, a seller, a shift?"

Then structure it

"I'd work it in three phases: diagnose in days one to three, fix what's cheap in week one, and protect peak in weeks two and three."

Diagnose. "First I'd segment the misses rather than treat 82% as one number — by shift, by day of week, by category, by order type. Thirteen points of miss almost never spreads evenly. Then I'd find where in the chain the time actually goes: is the order sitting in the bank before picking starts, is picking slow, is packing the bottleneck, or are we finishing on time and missing the carrier handover? Those four have completely different fixes and only measuring the stages tells you which."

Fix the cheap things immediately. "In week one I'd only do things that need no budget and no approval: releasing the order bank earlier in the day, re-sequencing so at-risk orders go first rather than first-in-first-out, moving the top-velocity SKUs into a forward-pick zone to cut walking distance, pre-staging packing materials, and putting in a mid-shift checkpoint so we escalate at midday instead of discovering the problem at cut-off."

Protect peak. "In parallel — because three weeks is not enough time to fix a structural problem — I'd plan for the volume rather than pretend I'll have solved it. Labour plan against a forecast rather than against last year's headcount, temporary staff trained and on the floor at least a week before peak so they're productive when it hits, confirm carrier capacity and cut-offs in writing now, and agree in advance with the commercial team what we deprioritise if we have to choose."

Close on measurement and honesty

"I'd track it daily against a target line, not weekly, because in peak a weekly number is an autopsy. And I'd be honest with the business early about what's achievable — if the diagnosis says 95% isn't reachable in three weeks, I'd say so and commit to a number I can actually hold, rather than promise 95% and miss it. Managing the expectation is part of managing the operation."

Why this works: the phasing (diagnose / cheap fixes / protect) is a reusable structure you can apply to almost any operations case. And the closing point about committing to an achievable number is the kind of thing that makes an experienced hiring manager visibly relax.
26. Return rate in one category has doubled month over month. Walk me through your investigation.
Say this

"First I'd check it's real before I chase it. A doubling can be a denominator artefact — if order volume in that category dropped, or a promotion shifted the mix, the rate moves without anything actually getting worse. So: is return volume up, or just the rate? And is it a reporting change — did we alter how returns are logged or when they're counted?"

"Assuming it's real, I'd narrow it down before forming any theory. Is it the whole category or a handful of SKUs? One seller or many? Did it start on a specific date — because a step change points at an event, a new listing, a new batch, a packaging change, a new carrier, while a gradual rise points at drift. And is it one region, which would point at transit or last mile rather than the product."

"Then the cause codes, which is the fastest route if the coding is disciplined. Is this 'not as described', which is a listing problem; 'damaged', which is packaging or transit; 'wrong item', which is my picking; 'changed mind', which is often a pricing or promotion effect and not an operational problem at all. Each of those has a different owner and I'd want to know which before I involve anyone."

"I'd also physically go and look at returned units. The data tells you what's happening and the units tell you why — I've found more root causes by opening returned parcels than by reading a report."

"Then I'd act on the dominant cause and, critically, put a control in so it doesn't recur: if it's a listing, correct it and check comparable listings from the same seller; if it's packaging, change the requirement and inspect against it at inbound; if it's my picking, it's an accuracy and training issue on specific SKUs. And I'd report back with the cause and the fix, not just the recovered number — otherwise nobody believes it won't come back."

Why this works: "check the denominator before you chase it" is an analyst's instinct and almost nobody says it in a supply chain interview. "Go and look at the units" is an operator's instinct. Having both is your entire differentiation in one answer.
27. Your last-mile partner is consistently missing SLA. You can't replace them. What now?
Say this

"I'd start by owning my side of it honestly, because in my experience a meaningful share of what gets called a carrier failure starts upstream. Am I handing over at the last possible minute so they have no recovery time? Is my address data clean? Are my parcels labelled to their standard? Am I giving them a smooth volume or a spike they can't staff for? Fixing those costs me nothing and it means that when I do push, I'm pushing from a position they can't deflect."

"Then I'd change the nature of the performance conversation. 'Your SLA is 84%' produces defensiveness. 'These are your failures segmented by zone, by cause and by day, and here are the three zones producing most of it' produces action — partly because it's useful to them, and partly because it's specific enough that they can't attribute it to volume in general."

"Structurally I'd want a weekly review with named owners and actions, not a monthly report; a joint improvement plan on the two or three biggest drivers rather than a long list; and a defined escalation path so I'm not relying on goodwill."

"And I'd reduce my dependency where I can without replacing them — routing a specific segment differently, changing the cut-off on the problem zones, or reducing the parcels that were always going to fail. If after all that the performance still doesn't move, then I've built a documented evidence base, and the conversation about whether they're really irreplaceable becomes a commercial one that someone above me can have with actual numbers in hand."

28. Reduce your cost per order by 15%. Where do you start?
Say this

"I'd start by breaking the cost per order into its components, because 15% of a blended number is meaningless until you know what it's made of. Typically: labour across receiving, picking, packing and dispatch; packaging materials; storage and space; transport; and then the failure costs — returns, re-deliveries, write-offs. That last bucket is the one people forget to count and it's very often where the money is."

"Then I'd rank by size and by how hard each is to move, and go after the intersection of big and feasible. From what I've seen, the order is usually this."

"Failure cost first, because it's pure waste — nobody wanted it. Every avoided return is the outbound leg, the return leg, the re-handling, the inspection and often the write-off. Cutting the return rate through listing accuracy and inbound quality control is the highest-return work available, and it improves customer experience at the same time, which almost no other cost lever does."

"Labour productivity second, and specifically travel time — in most warehouses picking travel is the largest single labour cost. Slotting fast-movers near dispatch, batch picking where the order profile allows it, and killing the micro-waste like walking for packing materials. These are process changes, not headcount cuts."

"Packaging third — right-sizing. Oversized boxes cost twice: the material and the volumetric shipping weight."

"Transport fourth — consolidation, better zone routing, and reducing re-attempts, since a second delivery attempt is close to doubling the cost of that order's last mile."

"What I'd push back on is cutting quality control to save inspection labour. It looks like a saving on the line item and it reliably costs more downstream in returns and refunds. I'd want to make that argument with the numbers rather than as an opinion — which is exactly the kind of analysis I'd build in the first month."

Why this works: you structured before you solved, you ranked by impact and feasibility, you avoided the naive "cut headcount" answer, and you pushed back on a false economy with a reason. The pushback is what marks you as a manager rather than an executor.

5The hard questions

29. You've been out of supply chain for over two years. Isn't your knowledge stale? Hard
Say this

"I'd challenge the premise slightly, and then concede the real part of it."

"What I've been doing for two years is building the internal platforms that operations teams at Jumia run on — reporting systems, data models, workflows. So I haven't left the domain, I've been looking at it from the systems side. I've stayed close to how the operation works because it's the subject matter of what I build."

"The honest concession is the tactile part: I haven't been on a warehouse floor daily for two years, and I'd want the first few weeks to be spent there rather than in an office. The physical rhythm of an operation is something you have to re-enter, and I wouldn't pretend otherwise."

"But the fundamentals of this job don't move that fast. Receiving discipline, inspection, inventory accuracy, working backwards from cut-off, root cause on returns — none of that is different in 2026 from 2023. What has changed is how much of the job is data, and that's the part I spent the two years getting much better at."

Why this works: conceding the true part makes the rebuttal credible. A candidate who claims nothing was lost sounds defensive; a candidate who says "here's exactly what I'd need to re-acquire, and here's what I gained instead" sounds honest and self-aware.
30. Why should we hire you over someone who's done this continuously for seven years? Hard
Say this

"If you need someone who can walk in and run a steady-state operation to the standard it's already at, they might genuinely be the better hire, and I'd say so."

"Where I'd back myself is if you want the operation to be better than it is. I've done the core job — nearly five years, end to end, including the reverse chain, which a lot of fulfillment managers have never owned. On top of that I bring something that's rare in this pool: I can build the analysis myself. I don't file a ticket and wait two weeks to find out which sellers are driving my returns. I write the query, build the report, and act on it this week."

"That's the difference between an operation that's managed and one that's improving. And it compounds — every month I'm here, the reporting behind the operation is better than the month before, and decisions get made on evidence rather than on whoever's most confident in the meeting."

Why this works: conceding the scenario where the other candidate wins makes you credible rather than arrogant, and it lets you define the terms on which you win. Never claim to be better at everything.
31. What's your biggest weakness?
Say this

"I go too deep into the data before I act. It's the flip side of what I'm good at — my instinct is to get the full picture before I move, and in an operation that's sometimes exactly wrong, because a decision today at 80% confidence beats a perfect decision on Friday."

"I noticed it during a peak where I spent a morning analysing a backlog that I should have spent reallocating people. The analysis was correct and it arrived too late to matter."

"What I do about it now is put a clock on it. If it's an operational decision inside the shift, I give myself a fixed window, act on what I have, and do the full analysis afterwards to confirm or correct. If it's a structural decision, then the depth is appropriate and I use it. Separating those two has been the fix — it hasn't gone away, but it's managed."

Why this works: it's a real weakness with a real cost, it's specific, and it has a working mitigation. It's also structurally safe — over-analysis is a flaw an ops employer can live with far more easily than "I'm disorganised."
Never say: "I'm a perfectionist", "I work too hard", "I care too much." Every interviewer has heard these hundreds of times and scores them as evasion.
32. Where do you see yourself in five years? Really asking: will you leave?
Say this

"Running a larger operation — more sites or a broader scope — and having built the reporting and control layer underneath it so decisions are made on evidence. Head of operations or supply chain director is the shape of it, but I care less about the title than about the scope of the chain I own."

"I'd also say the honest thing behind that question: I know my CV raises whether I'll switch again. My answer is that I made the switch deliberately, I got exactly what I went for, and I'm coming back with it on purpose. This isn't me trying something. Seven years in one company should tell you I don't move for the sake of moving."

Why this works: naming the unasked question is disarming and it's far better than leaving it unaddressed, where it quietly costs you the offer. The tenure point is your strongest evidence — use it here.
33. What's your management style?
Say this

"Clear standards, visible numbers, no surprises. I want everyone on the team to know what good looks like today and where we are against it — I put the target and the actual somewhere they can see it, because a team that can't see the score can't play."

"I'm hands-on in the sense that I'll be on the floor during peak and I'll pick if we're short. I'm not hands-on in the sense of doing people's jobs for them — I'd rather train someone through an error twice than take the task off them."

"On performance, I go to the process before I go to the person. When someone's numbers are off, I look at their error pattern first, because most of the time you find a process trap rather than a motivation problem. And if it genuinely is about the person, I have that conversation early and directly — letting someone fail slowly is worse for them than a hard conversation in week two."

"The thing I'm most deliberate about is feedback in both directions. The people picking and packing know where the process is broken long before it shows up in my report. If they don't tell me, that's my failure, not theirs."

6The questions you ask them

Ask three to five. Interviewers consistently report that the questions a candidate asks move their assessment more than any single answer. These are ordered — lead with the first two.

Ask these

  • "What does this operation's dispatch SLA and return rate look like today, and where do you want them in twelve months?" — Signals you think in KPIs immediately, and tells you whether you're inheriting a fire or a steady state.
  • "If I'm successful, what will be different in six months that isn't true today?" — The single best question in any interview. It forces them to describe the actual mandate.
  • "Where does this operation lose the most money today — is it labour, transport, or failure costs?" — Very few candidates ask about failure cost. It marks you out.
  • "What's the reporting situation? Do you have reliable operational data, or is a lot of it still manual?" — Sets up your differentiator without you boasting, and the answer tells you how much of your first year would be spent fixing data.
  • "Who would I work with most outside my own team — commercial, last mile, customer service? And how does that relationship work today?" — Cross-functional friction is where ops jobs actually fail.
  • "What's the team like right now — size, tenure, any gaps you're trying to fill?"
  • "What happened to the last person in this role?" — Direct, entirely fair, and the answer is often the most useful information you'll get all day.
Don't ask in round one: holidays, working from home, hours, or "what's the next promotion". All legitimate — all for after you have the offer.

7Salary, closing, and the follow-up

When they make the offer

Never accept on the call. Even if it's great.

Say this

"Thank you — I'm genuinely pleased, and I want to say clearly that I want this role. Could you send the details in writing so I can look at the full package properly? I'll come back to you within [48 hours / two days]."

Then, if you're negotiating:

Say this

"I've read through everything and I want to accept — the scope is exactly what I'm looking for. The one thing I'd like to discuss is the base. Given that I'm bringing the analytics and systems side alongside the operational experience, and based on what I've seen in the market at this scope, I was targeting [X]. Is there room to move on the base? If the base is genuinely fixed, I'm open to looking at [the bonus / a review at six months / an allowance] instead."

Why this works: you lead with acceptance so they know the deal isn't at risk, you ask for one thing rather than three, you justify it, and you offer an alternative route if the base is locked. That last part is what stops a negotiation turning into a standoff.

Your closing statement — when they say "anything you'd like to add?"

Say this

"Just one thing. I know my CV is an unusual shape, so I want to be direct about what you'd be getting. You'd get someone who has genuinely run this job end to end — inbound through to returns, with a team — not someone who has supervised part of it. And you'd get someone who can build the analysis to improve it instead of waiting for a report."

"I'm not looking to try operations. I've done it for five years, I went and got the technical skill specifically because the operation needed it, and this is where I want to use it. I'd really like this role."

Why this works: it addresses their doubt one final time, restates your two differentiators, and ends with explicit enthusiasm. Saying plainly that you want the job measurably increases offer rates — most candidates never do it.

8Full mock interview — read this out loud

A realistic 20-minute segment with a sceptical hiring manager. Note how they interrupt, probe, and push back — that's what real interviews do, and it's what you need to rehearse. Read both parts aloud at least once.

Round 2 · Hiring manager · Head of Operations
Interviewer

Thanks for coming in, Syrine. I've read your CV and I'll be honest — I'm a bit puzzled by it. Let's start there. You're a software developer. Why are you sitting in a supply chain interview?

You

It's a fair question and I'd rather answer it first than have it sitting in the room all hour.

I ran fulfillment at Jumia for nearly five years — end to end for our third-party sellers. I didn't leave because I disliked it. I left because of one specific frustration: I could see a problem clearly in the data and I couldn't fix the system that caused it. I'd know our returns were spiking on a category because of bad listings, and then I'd raise a ticket and wait weeks.

So I went and learned to build the systems. Two and a half years later I've got what I went for — I can get an answer out of a database myself instead of requesting a report. And what I've realised is I don't want to build tools for other people's operations. I want to run the operation.

Interviewer

Hmm. My concern is you'll be bored in six months and go back to a developer salary.

You

I'd be worried about that too if I were you. Two things I'd offer.

One — I've already made that move once, deliberately, so I'm not guessing about what's on the other side. That's exactly why I'm confident about this one. Someone who's never tried tech might romanticise it. I know what it is.

Two — seven years at one company. I don't move for the sake of moving.

And on boredom, honestly, the operational work is the part I found more engaging. A bad shift is a problem you solve in the next two hours with the people in front of you. That's not something I found on the engineering side.

Interviewer

All right. Tell me about the operation. Give me the scale — I want numbers.

You

Roughly [Y] orders a day, across [X] active sellers and [Z] SKUs, team of [N] across [number] shifts. Peak campaigns took us to [multiple]× that.

My scope was the full chain: inbound receiving and put-away, quality inspection, inventory accuracy, picking, packing, dispatch planning, carrier handover, and the entire reverse chain — returns, failed deliveries, grading, restock-or-reject, seller reimbursement. Plus seller performance management operationally.

Interviewer

The reverse chain is interesting, most people gloss over that. What did you actually do with returns?

You

The thing I'd point to is that I made every return get a root-cause code. Not free text — a fixed code list, applied consistently.

That sounds administrative and it was the single most valuable thing I did, because it turned returns from a pile of anecdotes into a dataset. Once I had a few months of it in Power BI, I could see the whole thing broken down by cause, category and seller — and it was heavily concentrated. A small group of sellers were driving a share of returns far out of proportion to their share of orders.

Interviewer

[interrupting] And what was the dominant cause? Damage?

You

No — and that was the surprise. Everyone assumed damage, and the proposal on the table at the time was to add outbound inspection.

The dominant cause was the product not matching the listing. Customers getting something that wasn't what they thought they'd ordered, and refusing it at the door or sending it back. Damage in transit was a minority.

Which mattered a lot, because outbound inspection would have added cost to every single order to address a minority cause. We killed that proposal and fixed the listings instead.

Interviewer

Did the return rate actually move, or is this a nice story?

You

It moved — [by X over Y months]. I'll be straight with you that I can't isolate how much of that was the listing corrections versus the packaging changes versus the seller corrective plans, because we did them in parallel during a peak period. I wouldn't claim a clean attribution.

What I'd stand behind fully is the structural change: returns went from something people complained about in a meeting to a monthly number with named causes and named owners. That's what made every subsequent improvement possible.

Interviewer

[nods] Okay. Let me give you a scenario. You start here Monday. Dispatch SLA is 82%, target is 95%, and peak is three weeks out. Go.

You

Can I ask two things first? Has it been at 82% for a while or has it dropped recently? And are the misses spread evenly or concentrated in a segment?

Interviewer

It's been drifting down over about four months. We haven't segmented it.

You

Then segmenting it is my first three days, because a four-month drift with no segmentation almost always means the 82% is hiding something much worse in one part of the operation and something fine everywhere else.

I'd cut it by shift, by day of week, by category and by order type. And separately I'd measure where the time actually goes in the chain — is the order sitting in the bank before picking starts, is picking slow, is packing the bottleneck, or are we finishing on time and missing the carrier handover? Those four have completely different fixes.

Week one, in parallel, I'd do only the things that cost nothing and need no approval: release the order bank earlier, re-sequence so at-risk orders go first instead of first-in-first-out, move top-velocity SKUs into a forward-pick zone, pre-stage packing materials at the stations, and put in a mid-shift checkpoint so we escalate at midday rather than discovering the miss at cut-off.

Weeks two and three I'd stop trying to fix it and start protecting peak — labour planned against a forecast, temps trained and on the floor a week early so they're actually productive, carrier capacity and cut-offs confirmed in writing.

And I'd tell you early what's realistic. If the diagnosis says 95% isn't reachable in three weeks, I'd come to you with a number I can actually hold rather than promise 95% and miss it.

Interviewer

Most people tell me they'd hit 95%.

You

Most people haven't been the person explaining at the end of peak why they didn't.

Interviewer

[laughs] Fair. Last one — what would you want to know from me?

You

Three things. First, what's the reporting situation — do you have reliable operational data, or is a lot of it still manual and in spreadsheets? That changes what my first three months look like more than anything else.

Second, where does this operation lose the most money today: labour, transport, or failure costs — returns, re-deliveries, write-offs? I ask because failure cost is usually the biggest and the least measured.

And third — if I'm successful here, what's different in six months that isn't true today?

End of segment
