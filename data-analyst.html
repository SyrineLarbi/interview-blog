Data Analyst

Your strongest combination on paper. You didn't just report numbers — you defined the KPI set an operation was run on, built the Power BI behind it, and then went and learned to write the SQL underneath. Most analyst candidates have the tools and no business context. You have both.

Your positioning for this role

There are two kinds of analyst candidate. The first knows SQL and pandas and has never owned a business outcome. The second understands the business and needs someone else to pull the data. You are the rare third case, and you should say so in almost exactly these words:

"I've been the person who owned the KPI, the person who built the report, and now the engineer who builds the data model underneath it. I know what it costs when the number is wrong, because I've been the one held to it."

The gap to close: you have never had "Data Analyst" on a business card. Expect them to test whether your SQL is real. It is — you write it daily and you have a genuine query-optimisation war story most analysts don't. Lead with the evidence, not with the job title.

1. Tell me about yourself.
Say this

"My route into analytics came through the business rather than through a data team, and I think that's my strength."

"I spent nearly five years as a Supply Chain Manager at Jumia, running fulfillment for third-party sellers. Part way through, the central team asked me to help audit and improve the operational processes across sites — and that's where the analytics came from. I defined the KPI set we measured ourselves against: dispatch SLA, picking accuracy, inventory accuracy, defect rate, return rate, failed-delivery rate, order cycle time. Then I built the Power BI reports and Excel Power Query dashboards behind them, which became what management used to plan, analyse and make decisions."

"The analysis that mattered most was on returns. I introduced root-cause coding on every return, which turned a pile of anecdotes into a dataset. Cutting that by cause, category and seller showed the dominant cause wasn't transit damage as everyone assumed — it was products not matching their listings, and it was concentrated in a small number of sellers. That killed a proposed outbound-inspection programme that would have added cost to every order to fix a minority cause, and redirected us to the listings instead."

"The move into engineering came out of the same pattern. The central team needed a help centre built for the agents, the dev team had no capacity, and I'd been doing a full-stack diploma alongside the job — so I offered to build it. When it shipped they proposed I join them, and since 2024 I've been writing SQL and doing relational data modeling daily, building the internal reporting platforms operations teams run on."

"So I've gone from owning the KPIs, to building the reporting, to building the data model underneath. That full path is what I'd bring to an analyst role."

Why this works: it has a narrative arc (consumer → owner → builder), a concrete analysis with a counter-intuitive finding and a business consequence, and it explains the engineering detour as analytical ambition rather than a career wobble. About 85 seconds.

3. Walk me through a piece of analysis you're proud of, start to finish.
Say this

"The returns root-cause analysis, because it changed a decision that was about to be made wrongly."

The question. "Return rate was rising and it was being treated as a warehouse quality problem. The proposal on the table was to add an outbound inspection step. I wasn't convinced, because our outbound packing checks weren't finding much — so I wanted to know what was actually causing returns before we added labour to every order."

Getting the data. "The honest answer is that the data didn't exist in a usable form. Returns were logged with free-text notes, which is unanalysable. So the first thing I did was change the collection: a fixed reason-code list, short enough that people wouldn't default to 'other', worded the way the team actually spoke. That's the step I'd emphasise — a lot of analysis work is upstream of the analysis."

The analysis. "After a few months I had consistent data. I built it in Power BI cut three ways: by cause code, by category, and by seller — and crucially, seller return rate against the category average, not absolute, because a 12% return rate is alarming in electronics and normal in fashion. An absolute threshold would have flagged all the wrong sellers."

The finding. "Transit damage was a minority. The dominant cause was product-not-as-described, and the distribution across sellers was heavily skewed — a small group produced a share of returns far out of proportion to their share of orders."

The outcome. "The outbound inspection proposal was dropped. Instead: listings corrected for the worst offenders, packaging requirements tightened where transit damage was genuinely the cause, and formal corrective plans with offboarding as the endpoint for the concentrated sellers. Return rate moved [by X]. But the durable outcome was that returns stopped being an anecdote and became a monthly number with named causes and named owners."

Why this works: it follows the exact shape a senior analyst wants to hear — question, data quality problem, method, finding, decision changed. The "I had to fix the collection first" section is what separates someone who has done real analysis from someone who has done exercises on clean datasets.

2Round 2 — SQL and technical

Expect a live SQL exercise, usually shared-screen. Talk while you write. A correct silent answer scores lower than a slightly imperfect one where they could follow your reasoning. Always state your assumptions about the schema before you start typing.

6. Explain the difference between INNER JOIN, LEFT JOIN and the joins people get wrong.
Say this

"INNER JOIN keeps only rows that match on both sides. LEFT JOIN keeps every row from the left table and fills nulls where there's no match on the right. RIGHT JOIN is the mirror of that and I almost never use it — it's clearer to reorder the tables and write LEFT. FULL OUTER keeps everything from both sides."

"The mistake I watch for in practice is using INNER JOIN when you meant LEFT, and silently losing rows. If I'm joining orders to returns to compute a return rate, an INNER JOIN gives me only orders that were returned — so my rate comes out as 100% and looks like a catastrophe. That's a real error I watch for, because it doesn't throw; it just gives you a confident wrong number."

"The other one is a LEFT JOIN quietly turning into an INNER JOIN because someone put a condition on the right-hand table in the WHERE clause. WHERE r.status = 'completed' filters out the nulls, so the LEFT JOIN is undone. The fix is to move that condition into the ON clause."

"And fan-out: if the right side has multiple matching rows, your left rows duplicate and any SUM downstream is inflated. That's the bug that produces revenue figures that are too high, so I check the grain of a join before I aggregate over it."

Why this works: anyone can recite the definitions. Naming the three ways joins go wrong in production — silent row loss, WHERE undoing a LEFT JOIN, and fan-out inflating sums — is what distinguishes someone who has actually shipped queries.
7. Live SQL: find the sellers with the highest return rate this year. Write it out
Say this first — before typing

"Let me state my assumptions. I'll assume an order_items table at one row per item shipped, a returns table keyed on order_item_id, and a sellers lookup. I need a LEFT JOIN so sellers with no returns still appear, and I'll want a minimum volume threshold — otherwise a seller who shipped one item and had it returned comes top at 100%, which is noise."

SELECT
    s.seller_id,
    s.seller_name,
    COUNT(DISTINCT oi.order_item_id)                        AS items_shipped,
    COUNT(DISTINCT r.order_item_id)                         AS items_returned,
    ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT r.order_item_id)
          / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT oi.order_item_id), 0), 2) AS return_rate_pct
FROM order_items   oi
JOIN sellers       s ON s.seller_id = oi.seller_id
LEFT JOIN returns  r ON r.order_item_id = oi.order_item_id
WHERE oi.shipped_at >= DATE '2026-01-01'
GROUP BY s.seller_id, s.seller_name
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT oi.order_item_id) >= 100
ORDER BY return_rate_pct DESC
LIMIT 10;
Then narrate these three choices out loud

"NULLIF on the denominator so I get a null rather than a divide-by-zero error."

"HAVING with a volume floor of 100 — that's the business judgement in this query. Without it the top of the list is all tiny sellers and the result is useless. I'd agree the actual threshold with whoever's using it."

"100.0 rather than 100 — in some engines integer division silently truncates your rate to zero. Cheap habit, saves an embarrassing dashboard."

"One caveat I'd flag to the stakeholder: this measures returns against items shipped in the same window, but a return lags the shipment. Late in the period the rate is understated because recent shipments haven't had time to come back. If this were going on a dashboard I'd either cohort it by shipment month or cut the window short by the typical return window."

That last caveat is the single highest-scoring thing you can say in a SQL interview. It shows you think about whether the number is true, not just whether the query runs. Most candidates never mention the lag.
8. Explain window functions and give me a use case.
Say this

"A window function computes across a set of rows related to the current row, without collapsing them the way GROUP BY does. So you keep every row and add the aggregate alongside it — that's the whole point. The moment you want a row and its context together, you want a window."

"The ones I use most: ROW_NUMBER, RANK and DENSE_RANK for top-N-within-group and for deduplication; LAG and LEAD for period-over-period comparison; and SUM OVER for running totals or for a share-of-total on the same row."

"A real example from my own work — I wanted the top five SKUs by returns within each category, with each one's share of its category's returns. You can't do that with GROUP BY alone, because you need the category total sitting next to the individual SKU row."

WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT
        category,
        sku,
        returns_30d,
        RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY returns_30d DESC)   AS rank_in_category,
        ROUND(100.0 * returns_30d
              / SUM(returns_30d) OVER (PARTITION BY category), 1)       AS pct_of_category
    FROM sku_returns
)
SELECT *
FROM ranked
WHERE rank_in_category <= 5
ORDER BY category, rank_in_category;
Add this detail — it scores well

"Note I wrap it in a CTE and filter outside. You can't put a window function in a WHERE clause, because WHERE is evaluated before the window. Some engines have QUALIFY for exactly this, but Postgres and MySQL don't, so the CTE is the portable way."

"On RANK versus ROW_NUMBER versus DENSE_RANK: RANK leaves gaps after a tie — 1, 2, 2, 4. DENSE_RANK doesn't — 1, 2, 2, 3. ROW_NUMBER forces an arbitrary order with no ties, which is what you want for deduplication but not for a leaderboard, because it silently picks a winner between two equal rows."

Have this one ready too — month-over-month with LAG:

SELECT
    month,
    orders,
    LAG(orders) OVER (ORDER BY month)                          AS prev_month,
    ROUND(100.0 * (orders - LAG(orders) OVER (ORDER BY month))
          / NULLIF(LAG(orders) OVER (ORDER BY month), 0), 1)   AS mom_growth_pct
FROM monthly_orders
ORDER BY month;
9. How would you find and remove duplicate rows?
Say this — and ask the question first

"First I'd ask what counts as a duplicate, because that's a business question, not a technical one. Two rows with the same email might be a genuine duplicate, or a customer who ordered twice. I need the definition before I delete anything."

"To find them, group by the key and filter for counts above one:"

SELECT customer_email, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM customers
GROUP BY customer_email
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
ORDER BY n DESC;
Then for keeping one of each

"To keep a specific one — usually the most recent — I'd use ROW_NUMBER, because it gives me control over which row survives rather than an arbitrary pick:"

WITH numbered AS (
    SELECT
        *,
        ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
            PARTITION BY customer_email
            ORDER BY created_at DESC, customer_id DESC
        ) AS rn
    FROM customers
)
SELECT * FROM numbered WHERE rn = 1;
Close on this

"Two things I'd add. The tiebreaker on customer_id matters — if two rows share a timestamp, without it the result is non-deterministic and your report changes between runs for no visible reason. And I'd want to know why there are duplicates before I clean them, because if it's an ingestion bug I'll be doing this every week. Cleaning the symptom is the short-term fix; the real fix is upstream."

10. What's the difference between WHERE and HAVING? And GROUP BY pitfalls?
Say this

"WHERE filters rows before aggregation. HAVING filters groups after aggregation. So you can't use an aggregate in WHERE, and there's usually no reason to put a plain row condition in HAVING — it works in some engines but it's slower, because you've aggregated rows you were only going to throw away."

"Practical version: WHERE shipped_at >= '2026-01-01' narrows which rows go into the aggregation. HAVING COUNT(*) >= 100 drops the resulting groups that are too small to trust."

"The GROUP BY pitfall I actually watch for is nulls. NULL forms its own group, so if your seller_id is nullable you get a phantom row that people either ignore or, worse, treat as a real seller. I usually surface it deliberately with COALESCE rather than letting it sit there unlabelled — an unlabelled null group in a dashboard is how a data quality problem stays invisible for six months."

"The other is counting: COUNT(*) counts rows including nulls, COUNT(column) skips nulls in that column, and COUNT(DISTINCT column) is different again. Choosing the wrong one is one of the most common sources of a number that's quietly wrong."

11. A query is running slowly. Walk me through what you do. Your strongest answer

This is your best technical story in the whole interview. Most analyst candidates have never profiled a query. You have a production one with a number attached.

Say this

"I'll give you the method and then a real example."

"Method: reproduce it first, then read the execution plan rather than guessing. I'm looking for sequential scans where I expected an index lookup, the row counts at each step — especially where the planner's estimate diverges badly from actual, because that usually means stale statistics — and whether a join is exploding the row count before a later filter cuts it back down."

"The real one: we had a production leave-approval screen taking about fifty seconds to load. Managers were effectively blocked from approving anything. I reproduced it, isolated the cost to the query fetching the approval list, and read the plan. Three separate problems."

"One — join columns with no supporting index, so it was doing full table scans. Two — tables joined in whose columns were never actually used in the response; someone had added them at some point and nothing depended on them any more. Three — a SELECT pulling columns the screen didn't render."

"I added the indexes, dropped the unused joins, narrowed the projection. Fifty seconds to six on the same hardware — about an eight-fold improvement, with the result set verified identical before and after."

You will be asked "why is it still six seconds?" — this is the real test. Answer:

"Because the remaining time is dominated by result-set size rather than by a bad plan. The next steps would be pagination — keyset rather than OFFSET, since OFFSET degrades the deeper you go — caching the approval list, or denormalising the few hot fields. I stopped there because six seconds unblocked the users and the remaining work needed a product decision about pagination that wasn't mine to make alone."

Also be ready for:

  • "Any downside to adding indexes?" → "Yes — they cost write throughput and storage. You accept that when reads dominate, which they do on an approval screen people load constantly and write to occasionally."
  • "Did it change behaviour?" → "No. Same rows, verified against the previous output before shipping."
Why the "still six seconds" answer matters so much: naming what you deliberately didn't do, and why, is the clearest available signal of engineering maturity. A candidate who claims they solved it perfectly is either lucky or not telling the truth.
12. How would you model data for analytics? Explain star schema.
Say this

"A star schema is a central fact table holding the events you measure, surrounded by dimension tables holding the attributes you slice by. For an e-commerce fulfillment operation the fact table would be something like order items or shipments — one row per event, with the measures on it — and the dimensions would be date, seller, SKU or product, warehouse, carrier, customer."

"The reason you denormalise into that shape rather than querying the transactional tables directly is that analytical queries are wide scans with lots of joins, and a fully normalised OLTP schema makes those expensive and hard to write. A star schema trades some redundancy for far simpler, faster reads — and, just as importantly, for queries an analyst can actually read six months later."

"The decision I'd flag as the one that matters most is grain: exactly what one row of the fact table represents. One row per order, or per order item, or per shipment. Get that wrong and every aggregate downstream is subtly wrong — if your grain is order-item but you treat it as orders, your order count is inflated by basket size. I'd nail down the grain in writing before building anything."

"I'd also raise slowly changing dimensions if it's relevant: if a seller moves category, do historical orders keep the old category or inherit the new one? There's no universally correct answer, but there is a wrong one — deciding it by accident."

Why this works: "define the grain first" is the answer of someone who has built a model and been burned. Raising slowly changing dimensions unprompted is a senior signal.

3Round 3 — Statistics and experimentation

You don't need to be a statistician. You need to not say anything wrong, and to show you know where the traps are. Confidence about limits beats false precision.

13. Correlation and causation — give me a real example of getting this wrong.
Say this

"Correlation means two things move together. Causation means one produces the other. The gap between them is usually a confounder — a third thing driving both — or reverse causality, or just selection in how the data was collected."

"A real one from my own work: return rate and delivery speed were correlated — orders delivered slower were returned more. The tempting read is that slow delivery causes returns, so speed up delivery.

"But the confounder was category. The slow-delivering items were disproportionately in [category], which also had an inherently high return rate for completely unrelated reasons — sizing, in that case. Once I segmented by category the relationship largely disappeared. If I'd acted on the raw correlation I'd have spent money on logistics to fix a product listing problem."

"So in practice, when I see a correlation that suggests an expensive action, I look for the confounder before I present it — and if I can't rule one out, I say so explicitly rather than letting the chart imply causation on my behalf."

Why this works: textbook answers use ice cream and drowning. A real confounder from your own operation, with the money that would have been wasted, is far more convincing — and it's genuinely your experience.
14. Design an A/B test. Say we want to test free delivery over a threshold.
Say this

"I'd work through five things: the hypothesis, the metrics, the unit and split, the sample size and duration, and what could invalidate it."

"Hypothesis. Stated so it can be wrong: 'Offering free delivery above [threshold] increases average order value enough to more than cover the delivery cost we absorb.' Note that's deliberately not 'increases conversion' — the business question is about margin, not volume."

"Metrics. One primary metric decided in advance — I'd use revenue per visitor, or better, contribution margin per visitor, because AOV alone can rise while total profit falls. Then secondary metrics: conversion rate, average basket size, delivery cost per order. And guardrails: return rate and cancellation rate, because a threshold pushes people to add items they didn't really want, and those come back."

"Unit and split. Randomise at customer level, not session level, so the same person doesn't see both variants and get confused — that contaminates the measurement and annoys the customer. 50/50 unless there's a reason to limit exposure."

"Duration and size. I'd calculate the sample needed for the minimum effect worth detecting before starting — if the smallest commercially interesting lift needs more traffic than we have in a month, the test isn't worth running and I'd say that up front. I'd run at least one full week, ideally two, to cover the weekly cycle, and I'd fix the end date in advance rather than watching it daily and stopping when it looks good. Stopping early on a favourable reading is how you ship a result that isn't real."

"What could break it. Seasonality or a promotion running concurrently. Novelty effect, where behaviour shifts at first and reverts. Cross-contamination if a shared account or device sees both. And the big one here — the effect on returns might only show up weeks later, so measuring at the end of the test window could make it look better than it is."

Why this works: the guardrail metrics and the delayed-returns caveat are both drawn from your actual operational experience, and they're exactly what a generic candidate misses. "Fix the end date in advance" shows you know what peeking does.
15. Explain a p-value and statistical significance as if to a marketing manager.
Say this

"The way I'd put it to a non-technical stakeholder: the p-value answers 'if this change actually did nothing at all, how likely is it that I'd see a difference at least this big just from random variation?' If that's very unlikely — conventionally under 5% — we call it significant, meaning random noise is a poor explanation for what we're seeing."

"Then I'd immediately add the two caveats, because this is where people get misled."

"First, significant does not mean important. With a large enough sample, a 0.1% lift becomes statistically significant and is still commercially worthless. So I always report the effect size and a confidence interval alongside it — 'we're fairly confident the lift is somewhere between 2% and 6%' is far more useful to a decision than 'p equals 0.03'."

"Second, not significant doesn't mean no effect. It often just means the test was underpowered. The honest statement is 'we couldn't detect an effect,' not 'there is no effect' — and those lead to very different decisions."

"And I'd flag the multiple-comparisons trap: if we check twenty metrics, one will look significant by chance alone. That's why I insist on naming the primary metric before the test starts."

Never say "the p-value is the probability the result is due to chance" or "the probability the null hypothesis is true." Both are the classic misstatements and a quantitative interviewer will notice immediately. Say "if the change did nothing, how likely is data this extreme."
16. How do you handle missing data and outliers?
Say this — missing data

"First I want to know why it's missing, because the mechanism decides the treatment. Missing at random is an inconvenience. Missing for a reason is a finding. If delivery timestamps are absent only for failed deliveries, then dropping those rows doesn't just lose data — it systematically biases my delivery-time analysis toward successes and makes performance look better than it is."

"So: quantify it first — what percentage, and is it concentrated in a segment? A 2% scatter and a 2% that's entirely one warehouse are completely different problems."

"Then the options. Leave it and report on the subset, being explicit about coverage. Drop it, only if it's genuinely random and small. Impute — median rather than mean for skewed data, or a segment-specific value — but I'm cautious with imputation in business reporting, because a stakeholder can't see which numbers you invented. Or treat 'missing' as its own category, which is often the most honest option and occasionally the most informative."

"Whatever I choose, I state it on the output. A dashboard that silently drops 8% of orders is worse than no dashboard."

Say this — outliers

"I don't delete outliers by default. An outlier is either an error or the most interesting row in the dataset, and I want to know which before I touch it."

"An order with a delivery time of 400 hours might be a data entry error — or it might be a real parcel that sat in a depot for two weeks, which is exactly the case I should be investigating. I'd go and look at a few individual records rather than deciding statistically."

"If they're genuine but distorting a summary, I'd use median and percentiles instead of the mean — for delivery times I'd report median and p95 rather than average, because the average hides the tail and the tail is what generates complaints. That's usually better than removing the rows."

17. Your overall metric improved but you're suspicious. What do you check? Simpson's paradox
Say this

"I'd segment before I believe it, because an aggregate can move purely from a change in mix while every underlying segment stays flat — or even gets worse. That's Simpson's paradox, and it's not exotic; it happens routinely in operational data."

"Concretely: suppose overall return rate improves. Before I report that as a win I'd check whether the rate improved within each category, or whether we simply sold proportionally more of a low-return category this month. If it's mix, nothing actually got better — and if that mix shift was a one-off promotion, the number will snap back next month and I'll have to explain why I claimed a success."

"So my standard check on any aggregate movement is: decompose it. Did the rate change within segments, or did the weights change between them? I'd usually show both — the blended number people asked for, and the within-segment view underneath it."

"The same logic applies to any average that moves. Average order value rising because customers buy more is a different business than average order value rising because cheap items went out of stock."

Why this works: you named the phenomenon, explained the mechanism in business terms, and gave a concrete e-commerce example from your own domain. That's a complete answer in three sentences plus proof.

4Round 4 — BI, dashboards and tooling

18. How do you approach building a dashboard? Someone asks you for one — what do you do?
Say this

"I don't start with the dashboard. I start by asking what decision it's meant to support, because 'I need a dashboard' is almost never the real requirement."

"Three questions. What decision will you make differently depending on what this shows? How often will you look at it — daily, weekly, monthly? And what will you do when the number is bad? If nobody can answer the third one, the dashboard won't get used, and I'd rather find that out in a ten-minute conversation than after two weeks of building."

"Then I design for the audience, and that's a lesson I learned the hard way. I once built one dashboard meant to serve both the floor team and management, and neither used it. They need genuinely different products. The team needs today against target, right now, readable across a room, with almost nothing on it. Management needs trend, comparison and outliers — where are we deviating and why. Trying to serve both produces something too dense for one and too shallow for the other."

"On the build itself: headline number first, trend second, breakdown third, detail on drill-down rather than on the front page. Every metric defined somewhere visible, because half the arguments about dashboards are actually arguments about definitions. And a data-freshness timestamp — people need to know whether they're looking at this morning or last Tuesday."

"Then I'd go back two weeks later and ask whether they're using it. If they're not, that's my problem to fix, not theirs."

Why this works: "what will you do when the number is bad?" is a question that visibly impresses experienced stakeholders. Admitting the one-dashboard-for-two-audiences failure makes the rest credible.
19. What's your experience with Power BI, Excel, and Python?
Say this

"Power BI is where I did most of my operational reporting — report building, dashboards, and the data modeling behind them. That last part is the bit people underrate: most bad Power BI reports are bad because of the model, not the visuals. Relationships pointing the wrong way, or the wrong grain, and then you're fighting the tool with increasingly baroque measures."

"Excel — advanced, and specifically Power Query, which I used for anything that needed repeating. If I'm doing the same cleaning steps by hand twice, that's a Power Query job. Pivot tables and dashboards for the analysis that didn't justify a full report."

"SQL is where I work most now — daily, in a production codebase, including data modeling and query optimisation rather than just reading."

"Python — pandas for analysis that's awkward in SQL, and I've worked with scikit-learn-style modeling: I built an attrition-prediction service with XGBoost served through FastAPI, including the feature work and interpreting what the model was actually keying on. I wouldn't call myself a data scientist, but I'm comfortable in that territory and I know where the boundary of my competence is."

Why this works: the Power BI modeling point proves depth beyond clicking charts together. Naming the limit of your ML claim — "I wouldn't call myself a data scientist" — buys you credibility on everything else you claim.

5Round 5 — Business cases

Structure beats cleverness. Clarify → hypothesise → say how you'd test each → name what you'd do with the answer. Always ask a clarifying question first.

20. Orders dropped 20% week over week. You have an hour before the leadership meeting. Go.
Step 1 — validate before you investigate

"First, is it real? Before I chase a cause I'd rule out the boring explanations, because a surprisingly high proportion of dramatic drops are measurement artefacts. Did a tracking or logging change ship? Is a data pipeline partially failed, so I'm looking at incomplete data? Is the comparison week distorted — did last week contain a promotion or a public holiday? And is it 20% against last week only, or also against the same week last year? A single week-over-week comparison is a weak baseline."

Step 2 — localise it

"If it's real, I'd narrow it before theorising. Segment aggressively: by channel — app, web, mobile web; by geography; by category; by new versus returning customer; by device and OS; by payment method. And check the shape in time — a cliff on a specific day points at an event or a release, a gradual slide points at something structural like competition or seasonality."

"The value of this step is that it usually collapses the problem. If the whole drop is Android users in one country from Tuesday, I'm no longer investigating 'orders' — I'm investigating a release."

Step 3 — walk the funnel

"Then I'd follow the funnel to find which step broke: sessions, product views, add-to-cart, checkout started, payment attempted, order confirmed. If sessions are flat and checkout conversion collapsed, that's a site or payment problem. If sessions themselves dropped, it's demand or acquisition — a marketing spend change, a campaign that ended, an SEO or ranking change. Those two branches go to completely different teams, so identifying which is the most valuable single thing I can do in the hour."

Step 4 — what you actually say in the meeting

"With an hour, I'd walk in with: whether it's real, where it's concentrated, which funnel step broke, my leading hypothesis with the evidence for it, and explicitly what I don't yet know. I'd much rather say 'it's concentrated in mobile checkout since Tuesday's release, I'm 70% confident it's the release, here's what I need two more hours to confirm' than present a confident answer I can't stand behind. In an incident, the cost of a confident wrong diagnosis is that ten people spend a day fixing the wrong thing."

Why this works: "is it real?" first is the professional instinct and most candidates skip straight to causes. Stating your confidence level and what you don't know is what a hiring manager actually wants from an analyst in an incident.
21. We're launching next-day delivery. How would you measure whether it worked?
Say this

"I'd want to know the goal before the metric, because 'did it work' means different things depending on why we did it. Is this defensive — matching a competitor to stop losing customers? Is it growth — acquiring new ones? Or is it margin — charging a premium? Each implies a different success measure, and I've seen launches judged against a metric nobody agreed to in advance."

"Assuming it's growth, I'd structure it as one primary metric, supporting metrics, and guardrails."

"Primary: incremental contribution margin from customers exposed to the option. Not orders, not revenue — margin, because next-day delivery has a real cost per order and it's entirely possible to grow revenue into a loss."

"Supporting: attach rate — what share of eligible orders choose it; conversion rate on eligible baskets; AOV for those orders; and repeat rate among customers who used it, which is where the long-term value would show up if it exists."

"Guardrails — and this is where my operations background makes me suspicious. On-time delivery rate for the next-day promise specifically: a promise you miss is worse than a promise you never made, and it damages trust on the standard option too. Failed-delivery rate, because compressing the window means less flexibility for the customer to be home. Return rate. Cost per delivery. And warehouse dispatch SLA overall — a next-day cohort that has to be picked first can quietly degrade everything else in the building. I'd want that measured explicitly, because it's the failure mode nobody plans for."

"On method: staged geographic rollout rather than a national launch, so I have a comparison group and can measure incrementality rather than just before-and-after. And I'd set the measurement window past the return window, so returns are actually in the data before anyone declares victory."

Why this works: the guardrails section is yours alone. No analyst without warehouse experience thinks to measure whether a next-day cohort cannibalises the dispatch SLA of everything else. That single point makes the answer memorable.
22. You're the first analyst for an operations team. What do you build in your first 90 days?
Say this

"Days 1–30: understand and audit. I'd spend the first weeks talking to the people who'd use the output and, if there's a warehouse, standing in it. Then I'd audit what exists — which numbers are already reported, who trusts them, and where they disagree. In my experience the first real finding is always that two teams have different numbers for the same thing, and that has to be resolved before anything else is worth building."

"I'd also map what's actually available in the systems versus what people believe is available. Those differ more than anyone expects."

"Days 30–60: define and instrument. Agree a core metric set with written definitions — for a fulfillment operation I'd start from the set I built at Jumia: dispatch SLA, picking accuracy, inventory accuracy, defect rate, return rate, failed-delivery rate, order cycle time. The written definition matters as much as the metric; most disputes about a number are really disputes about its definition. Then build one reliable daily operational view before building anything clever."

"Days 60–90: find something. By this point I should be producing insight rather than reporting. My first target would be root-cause coding on failures — returns, failed deliveries, discrepancies — because that's consistently where the concentrated, actionable findings are, and it's the analysis that paid off best for me before. I'd expect to be able to show which small set of causes, categories or suppliers drives a disproportionate share of the cost."

"The thing I'd deliberately avoid is spending 90 days building a beautiful dashboard nobody asked for. I'd rather have one ugly daily report people actually open and one finding that changed a decision."

6Round 6 — Behavioral

23. Tell me about a time a stakeholder disagreed with your analysis. STAR
Say this

Situation. "My returns analysis showed that a specific high-volume seller was generating a share of returns far out of proportion to their share of orders. The commercial owner of that relationship disagreed — fairly, from their position, because the seller was real revenue and my finding implied a difficult conversation with them."

Task. "Get to a decision that was actually right for the business, rather than winning an argument."

Action. "First I took their challenge seriously instead of defending. Their specific objection was that the seller was in a naturally high-return category, which was a legitimate point — so I went back and re-cut it against the category average rather than as an absolute rate. The seller was still an outlier, but the gap was smaller than my first version had implied, and I said so."

"Then I changed what I was presenting. Instead of a return rate, I put a cost on it: the handling, the re-work, the write-offs. And I proposed a corrective plan with a review date rather than an offboarding recommendation, which gave them a route that didn't cost them the revenue immediately."

Result. "They backed it, and the seller's numbers improved over [period]. What I took from it is that a stakeholder pushing back is usually giving you free information about a weakness in your analysis — mine genuinely was less rigorous than it should have been, and their objection made it better. I also learned to lead with cost rather than with rate, because rate is a number you can argue about and cost is a number that ends the discussion."

Why this works: you conceded a real flaw and improved the work, rather than the standard "I showed them the data and they agreed." Interviewers are listening for whether you can be wrong gracefully — most candidates fail this one by being too victorious.
24. Tell me about a time your analysis was wrong. Honesty test
Say this

Situation. "Early on I reported a sharp improvement in one of our operational metrics and presented it as a genuine gain from a process change we'd made."

What went wrong. "It wasn't real. The improvement came from a change in mix, not from the process — the volume composition had shifted that month in a way that flattered the blended number. Every underlying segment was roughly flat. I'd looked at the aggregate, seen it move in the direction I expected because I'd just made a change I believed in, and not decomposed it."

Action. "I found it myself the following month when the number moved back and I went looking for why. I corrected it in the next review — proactively, before anyone asked — and explained exactly what I'd missed."

Result. "Uncomfortable, but it was the right call and it cost me nothing in credibility; arguably the opposite. The permanent change was that I now decompose any aggregate movement before I report it: did the rate change within segments, or did the weights change between them? And I'm particularly careful when a number moves the way I wanted it to, because that's exactly when I'm least likely to check."

Why this works: naming confirmation bias in yourself — "I'm most careful when the number moves the way I wanted" — is a genuinely sophisticated answer, and it links back to your Simpson's paradox answer, showing the lesson is real rather than rehearsed.
25. How do you explain a technical finding to a non-technical audience?
Say this

"I lead with the decision, not the method. Executives want the 'so what' in the first sentence — the analysis is supporting evidence, not the headline. So I'd open with 'we should stop doing X, here's why', not with 'I ran a segmentation across six dimensions.'"

"I translate into their currency. Return rate means nothing to a commercial director; cost and lost revenue mean everything. Same finding, different unit, completely different reception — I learned that directly when a returns finding I'd presented as a rate went nowhere, and the same finding presented as a cost got acted on within a week."

"I say what I'm not sure about. Non-technical audiences often assume analysis is certain, so if there's a caveat that could change the decision, it goes in the main message rather than a footnote. And if there's a caveat that wouldn't change the decision, I leave it out entirely — including every limitation you can think of is a way of hiding."

"And I use one chart, not five. If I can't make the point with one, I don't understand it well enough yet."

26. Three people want three urgent things. How do you prioritise?
Say this

"I'd ask each of them two questions: what decision does this feed, and when is that decision being made. That usually re-sorts the list by itself, because at least one 'urgent' request turns out to feed a meeting next month and one turns out to be needed in two hours."

"Then I'd weigh by decision value and by effort. A 20-minute query that unblocks a real decision beats a two-day analysis that's interesting, and I'd do the quick one first even if it's from the more junior requester."

"I'd also look for the hidden case: sometimes two of the three requests are the same underlying question asked differently, and I can serve both once."

"Then I'd be explicit and visible about the order rather than quietly doing them in sequence. 'I can get you this by Thursday; here's what I'm doing first and why — tell me if that's wrong.' Most conflicts over analyst time resolve themselves the moment the requesters can see each other's requests. If two genuinely can't both be met, that's an escalation, and I'd rather escalate on Monday with options than miss both on Friday."

27. What's your biggest weakness?
Say this

"I over-invest in getting the data right before I share anything. It comes from being on the receiving end of a wrong number — when it's your own KPI on a screen in front of your director, you become very reluctant to publish something you haven't checked three times."

"The cost is that I sometimes hold back a finding that was decision-ready at 90% confidence while I chase the last 10%, and by the time it's perfect the meeting has happened."

"What I do about it now is separate the two cases explicitly. If it's a recurring report or anything that becomes a definition people will rely on, the rigour is correct and I keep it. If it's a one-off question feeding a decision this week, I share the directional answer with the confidence level attached — 'here's what it looks like, here's what I haven't verified yet' — rather than waiting. Being explicit about confidence turned out to be the whole fix."

Don't say "I'm a perfectionist" without the cost and the fix. The structure that works is: real flaw → concrete cost → specific mechanism you use now.

  • "How much of this role is answering questions people bring you, versus finding the questions yourself?" — Tells you whether it's a reporting job or an analyst job. Ask early.

8Full mock interview — read out loud

A senior analyst or data lead interviewing you. They will test whether your SQL is real. Note how the pushback escalates.

Round 2 · Data Lead · technical screen
Interviewer

So, your background is supply chain and then software engineering. Help me understand what makes you an analyst.

You

I came to analytics through the business rather than through a data team, and I'd argue that's the useful direction.

At Jumia I ran fulfillment for third-party sellers. Part way through, the central team asked me to help audit and improve the processes across sites, and that's where the analytics came from — I defined the KPI set, dispatch SLA, picking accuracy, inventory accuracy, defect rate, return rate, failed-delivery rate, cycle time, and built the Power BI behind it. That became what management used to plan and make decisions.

The engineering move came the same way. They needed a help centre built for the agents, the dev team had no capacity, and I'd been doing a development diploma alongside the job — so I built it. When it shipped they offered me a place on the team, and I've been writing SQL daily for two and a half years since.

Interviewer

Let's test that. Here's a schema: order_items, returns, sellers. Give me the top sellers by return rate this year.

You

Let me state assumptions first. order_items is one row per item shipped, returns keys on order_item_id, sellers is a lookup.

I need a LEFT JOIN to returns — if I use an INNER JOIN I only get items that were returned and every seller comes out at 100%. And I want a minimum volume threshold, otherwise a seller who shipped one item that came back tops the list at 100% and the report is noise.

[writes] So: count distinct order items as the denominator, count distinct returns as the numerator, NULLIF on the denominator to avoid a divide-by-zero, multiply by 100.0 rather than 100 so integer division doesn't truncate it to zero, GROUP BY seller, HAVING at least a hundred items shipped, order descending.

Interviewer

Good. Why a hundred?

You

Honestly, it's a placeholder — that's a business decision, not a technical one, and I'd agree it with whoever's using the report. The principle is that below some volume the rate is dominated by variance rather than by seller behaviour.

There's actually a bigger caveat I'd raise before anyone puts this on a dashboard. This measures returns against items shipped in the same window, but returns lag shipment by days or weeks. So late in the period the rate is understated — recent shipments haven't had time to come back yet. If a seller onboarded in November, they'd look great in a December report purely because of the lag.

I'd either cohort it by shipment month and let each cohort mature, or cut the window short by the typical return window. Which one depends on whether they want it live or accurate.

Interviewer

[writes something down] Most people don't mention that. Okay — window functions. Do you use them?

You

Regularly. RANK and ROW_NUMBER for top-N-within-group and deduplication, LAG for period comparison, SUM OVER for running totals and share-of-total.

A real case: I wanted the top five SKUs by returns within each category, plus each SKU's share of its category's returns. GROUP BY can't do that, because I need the category total next to the individual row.

One thing worth mentioning — you can't filter on a window function in WHERE, since WHERE evaluates before the window. So I wrap it in a CTE and filter outside. Some engines have QUALIFY for that, Postgres and MySQL don't.

Interviewer

What's the difference between RANK and DENSE_RANK?

You

RANK leaves gaps after ties — 1, 2, 2, 4. DENSE_RANK doesn't — 1, 2, 2, 3. And ROW_NUMBER never ties, it just picks an order, which makes it right for deduplication and wrong for a leaderboard, because it silently chooses between two genuinely equal rows.

Interviewer

Fine. Scenario. Your dashboard shows return rate improved 3 points this month. The ops director is delighted. What do you check before you let them announce it?

You

Whether it's mix or whether it's real.

The blended rate can improve while every single category stays flat, purely because we sold proportionally more of a low-return category that month. So I'd decompose it: did the rate improve within segments, or did the weights change between them?

I'd also check whether anything changed in how returns are logged or counted, and whether the return lag is flattering the recent weeks.

I ask because I got this exact thing wrong once. Early on I reported an improvement as a genuine process gain, and it was mix. I found it myself the next month when it reverted. Now I decompose any aggregate movement before reporting it — and I'm especially careful when the number moves the way I wanted it to, because that's when I'm least likely to look.

Interviewer

Last thing. What's the hardest part of this job, in your view?

You

Getting the analysis used. The technical work is the easy part.

I've built a dashboard nobody opened — one I designed to serve both the floor team and management, and it served neither. And I've had a finding go nowhere presented as a rate, then get acted on within a week when I re-presented it as a cost.

So the hard part is understanding the decision well enough to answer the right question, in the right unit, for the right person. That's the bit I think my operations background actually gives me — I've been the stakeholder ignoring a report, so I know what it takes to not be ignored.
