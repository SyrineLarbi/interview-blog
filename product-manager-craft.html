0First — product manager is not project manager

Lesson 0The difference, and why it matters in this interview

A Product Manager decides what to build and why. A Project Manager makes sure it gets delivered — on time, with the right people.

Think of it like…

A restaurant opens a new branch.

The product manager asks: who eats here? What should be on the menu? Do we need a kids' menu? Should we deliver? Which dish do we drop because nobody orders it?

The project manager asks: when do the tables arrive? Is the kitchen ready before the opening date? Who is missing? What will make us late?

Both matter. They are different jobs.

Product ManagerProject Manager
Main questionAre we building the right thing?Will we finish on time?
OwnsThe problem, the priorities, the outcomeThe plan, the schedule, the risks
Success isUsers' lives got better and the business grewDelivered on time, in scope, in budget
Ends whenNever. The product keeps living.The project is finished.
Says"We are not building that.""That will take three more weeks."
Your job offer is Product Manager. So every answer should be about choosing and outcomes, not about schedules and Gantt charts. If you talk too much about planning and deadlines, they will think you misread the role.
If they ask you the difference — say this

"A product manager decides what to build and why. A project manager makes sure it gets delivered."

"I am accountable for the outcome — did this actually help the student and the business. Not for the schedule."

"In a small team one person often does both. But if I had to choose where my attention goes, it goes to whether we chose the right problem. Delivering the wrong thing on time is still a failure."

Don't say "they are basically the same." They are not, and this is a quick way for an interviewer to decide you don't know the job.

1Part 1 — What the job actually is

Lesson 1The five jobs of a Product Manager

Everything a PM does fits in five boxes. Learn the five. You can answer almost any question from them.

#The jobWhat it looks like in real life
1Find the real problem Talk to users. Look at data. Find out what is actually wrong — not what people say is wrong.
2Decide what to do first 20 good ideas, room for 3. Choose. Say no to 17 and explain why.
3Explain it clearly Write it down so engineers know what to build, and so the boss knows what will change.
4Help the team ship it Answer questions fast. Remove blockers. Make the small decisions so the team is not waiting.
5Measure and learn Did it work? How do we know? Keep it, fix it, or kill it.
The honest version

A PM has almost no power. You cannot order the engineers. You cannot order the designer. You cannot order the sales team.

You have only three things: a clear problem, evidence, and the ability to explain. That is the whole job. People follow you because your reasoning is good, not because of your title.

You have done all five at Jumia. You found the real cause of returns (1). You decided to stop the inspection plan (2). You explained it to the commercial team with numbers (3). You ran the fix (4). You measured the return rate after (5). Use this example — it covers all five boxes in one story.
Say this

"My job is five things: find the real problem, decide what we do first, explain it clearly, help the team ship it, and prove whether it worked."

"And I have no authority over anyone. People follow the reasoning, not the title. So the quality of my thinking is the whole job."

Lesson 2What a real week looks like

If they ask "what does a normal week look like for you?", this is the answer. It proves you understand the job in practice, not in theory.

MONDAY      Look at the numbers from last week. What moved? What broke?
            Sprint planning with the team — what we do for the next two weeks.

TUESDAY     User interviews. 2 or 3 students or teachers. Always be talking to users.
            Write up what you heard.

WEDNESDAY   Writing day. Specs, PRDs, decisions. This needs quiet time —
            protect it or it disappears.

THURSDAY    Stakeholders. Update the boss. Talk to sales/support/content.
            Answer the "when will X be ready" questions.

FRIDAY      Review what the team built. Test it yourself.
            Look ahead — what is coming in 4 to 8 weeks, and what will block it?

EVERY DAY   15-minute standup with the team.
            Answer team questions quickly — a blocked engineer is expensive.
The two things new PMs get wrong:
  • They stop talking to users. Meetings fill the week. Then you are deciding from opinion. Put user conversations in the calendar as a fixed appointment, every week, or they will not happen.
  • They answer the team too slowly. If an engineer asks you a question and waits two days, you just wasted two days of their work. Fast, clear answers are more valuable than perfect ones.
Say this

"My week has four fixed parts. Monday I look at the numbers and plan with the team. Tuesday I talk to users — I protect that time or it disappears. Wednesday I write. Thursday is stakeholders."

"And every day, I answer the team fast. A blocked engineer is the most expensive thing in the room."

Lesson 3The three questions behind every product decision

Before building anything, a PM answers three questions. If any answer is no, stop.

QuestionMeaningWho helps you
Do people want it?Is the problem real and painful? Will they use it?Users, research, data
Can we build it?Is it possible with our team, our time, our technology?Engineers
Should we build it?Does it help the business? Is it legal and safe?Business, legal
Think of it like…

Opening a shop. People must want what you sell. You must be able to get the stock. And you must make enough money to pay the rent.

Miss any one, and the shop closes. It does not matter how good the other two are.

For an AI product, add a fourth: is it reliable enough? An AI feature can be wanted, buildable and good for business — and still be wrong 20% of the time. In education, that fourth question can stop a project on its own. Say this if they ask about AI product decisions.
Say this

"Before we build I want three answers. Do people want it — is the problem real. Can we build it. And should we build it — does it help the business."

"For AI features I add a fourth: is it reliable enough. Something can be wanted and buildable and still be wrong too often to put in front of a child."

2Part 2 — Discovery: finding the real problem

This is the part most people skip. It is also the part that separates a good PM from a ticket writer.

Lesson 4How to talk to users — what to ask and what never to ask

Ask about the past, not the future. People are bad at predicting what they will do. They are good at remembering what they did.

Think of it like…

Ask someone: "Would you buy a healthy frozen meal?" Almost everyone says yes. It makes them feel good to say yes.

Now ask: "What did you eat for dinner last night? And the night before?" Now you get the truth.

❌ Never ask✅ Ask instead
"Would you use a feature that…?""Tell me about the last time you had this problem."
"Do you like this idea?""What did you do about it? Walk me through it."
"How often would you use it?""How many times did that happen last month?"
"What features do you want?""What is the most frustrating part of studying right now?"
"Would you pay for it?""What do you pay for today to solve this?"

Three rules while you talk:

  • Do not sell. The moment you explain your idea, they become polite. Polite people give you useless data.
  • Be quiet. After they answer, wait 3 seconds. The second thing they say is usually the real thing.
  • Ask "why" three times. "It's hard." Why? "I don't understand it." Why? "The video goes too fast and I can't stop and ask." Now you have something you can build.
How many people? 5 to 8 users per group is usually enough to see the pattern. You are not doing statistics. You are looking for the repeated story. If three people describe the same pain in the same words, that is real.
Say this

"I ask about the past, not the future. People cannot predict what they will do, but they remember what they did."

"So instead of 'would you use this', I ask 'tell me about the last time this happened, and what did you do'."

"And I do not describe my idea during the interview. The moment I do, they become polite, and polite answers are useless."

Lesson 5Personas and "jobs to be done"

A persona is a short description of one type of user. A job to be done is what they are really trying to achieve.

The famous example

People do not buy a drill because they want a drill. They want a hole. Actually they want a shelf. Actually they want a tidy room.

If you only think "they want a drill", you build a better drill. If you understand the real job, you might build something completely different — and better.

For the learning platform, the four users and their real jobs:

UserWhat they say they wantWhat they really want
Student"Help with my homework"To stop feeling stupid. To pass the exam. To finish quickly and go out.
Parent"A learning app"Proof their money is working. Less stress at home about school.
Teacher"A tool for my class"Less marking. To know who is struggling before the test, not after.
School"Better results"Numbers they can show to parents and to the ministry.
Be honest about the student. Most students are not excited about learning. They want to finish and go out. A good PM designs for the real person, not for a perfect imaginary student. Saying this in the interview shows maturity — many candidates describe a fantasy user.
Say this

"People do not want a drill. They want a hole. Actually they want a tidy room."

"So for a student, the real job is not 'learn maths'. It is 'stop feeling stupid, pass the exam, and finish quickly'. I would design for that real person, not for an imaginary student who loves studying."

"And the parent is usually the one paying. Their job is proof that the money is working. So the progress report is not a small feature — for them it is the product."

Lesson 6How do you know a problem is real?

One person complaining is a story. A pattern in the data is a problem. You need both.

The four sources, and what each is good for:

SourceTells youDoes not tell you
User interviewsWhy something happensHow often, how many people
Data / analyticsWhat and how manyWhy
Support ticketsThe loudest problemsThe silent ones — people who quit without complaining
Sales / teachersWhat blocks a dealWhat everyday users feel
The rule that matters: data tells you what, interviews tell you why. You need both. Data alone gives you "40% of students stop at lesson 3" and no idea what to do. Interviews alone give you one angry parent and no idea if it matters.
Watch out for the silent users

Support tickets only show you people who cared enough to complain. The students who opened the app, felt lost, closed it and never came back — they wrote no ticket.

They are usually the biggest group and the biggest problem. You only find them in the data.

Say this

"Data tells me what is happening and how big it is. Interviews tell me why. I need both — one without the other leads to bad decisions."

"And I look for the silent users. Support tickets only show people who complained. The ones who opened the app, felt lost and never came back wrote nothing. They are usually the bigger group."

Use your Jumia story here: "I saw this at Jumia. Returns were logged as free text, so nobody could analyse them. I changed the collection first — a fixed list of reasons. A few months later I had real data, and it showed the cause everybody assumed was wrong. Sometimes the first job is fixing how you collect information, before you can answer anything."

3Part 3 — Deciding what to build

Lesson 7Prioritisation — how to choose They will ask this

You have 20 ideas and room for 3. Prioritisation is how you choose, and how you explain the choice to people who disagree.

The simplest method — value vs effort:

            HIGH VALUE
                 │
   DO FIRST      │      DO IF YOU CAN
   (easy + big)  │      (big but hard)
   ──────────────┼──────────────────────  →  HIGH EFFORT
   DO LATER      │      DO NEVER
   (easy, small) │      (hard and small)
                 │

Two questions per idea: how much does it help, and how hard is it. Put them on the square. Start top left. This is enough for most decisions and takes 20 minutes.

The named method they may ask about — RICE:

R — ReachHow many people does this affect? (per month)
I — ImpactHow much does it help each one? (big / medium / small)
C — ConfidenceHow sure am I? (100% / 80% / 50%)
E — EffortHow much work? (person-months)
Score  =  Reach × Impact × Confidence
          ─────────────────────────────
                    Effort

Example — AI tutor for maths grade 8:
   Reach       8,000 students/month
   Impact      3  (big)
   Confidence  0.5  (we are not sure it improves learning)
   Effort      4 person-months
   Score = (8000 × 3 × 0.5) / 4 = 3,000
The honest part — say this and they will trust you. RICE looks scientific and it is not. You choose the numbers. Two people score the same idea very differently.

Its real value is not the score. It is that it forces the conversation into the open: "you said impact is high — based on what?" The argument becomes about evidence instead of about who speaks loudest. That is the actual benefit, and most candidates do not know it.

The two things every framework misses:

  • Some items unlock others. Building the map of topics (which subject comes before which) is not a feature anyone asked for. But three later features become cheap because of it. Frameworks score it low. You must argue for it separately.
  • Some items reduce risk. A two-week test to find out whether an AI approach even works is not "value". It is information. Sometimes buying information is the best use of two weeks.
Say this

"I start simple — how much does it help, how hard is it. That answers most decisions quickly."

"For bigger decisions I use RICE: how many people, how much impact, how confident am I, how much work."

"But I would be honest that RICE is not science. I choose the numbers. Its real value is that it forces us to say why we think impact is high. The argument becomes about evidence instead of who is most senior in the room."

"And I always fight for two things frameworks score badly: the boring foundations that make later work cheap, and short experiments that buy us information."

Lesson 8How to say no

Saying no is most of the job. Saying it badly is how PMs lose the room.

Think of it like…

"No" closes the door. "Not now, and here is what would change my mind" leaves the door open and keeps the relationship.

People can accept not getting their feature. They cannot accept feeling ignored.

The four-step no:

1. Show you understood"So the problem is that teachers can't see who is struggling until the test. Is that right?"
2. Give the real reason"We're not doing it this quarter because we're fixing the content map, and the teacher view depends on it."
3. Say what would change it"If we heard this from three more schools, or it blocked a deal, it moves up."
4. Give them something"In the meantime, here's a report you can run manually. Would that help for now?"
Never say: "It's not on the roadmap." That is not a reason, it is a wall. Also never say "the engineers say it's too hard" — you are hiding behind your team and it will get back to them.
Say this

"I never just say no. I repeat the problem back so they know I understood, give the real reason, say what would change my mind, and try to offer something small in the meantime."

"People can accept not getting their feature. They cannot accept feeling ignored."

Lesson 9The roadmap — and why dates are dangerous

A roadmap is a plan of problems we will solve, not a list of features with dates.

Think of it like…

Bad roadmap: "AI tutor — 15 March. Teacher dashboard — 30 April."

Now every date is a promise. If the AI tutor turns out to need three more months, you have failed — even if stopping was the right decision.

Good roadmap: "This quarter: students stop getting stuck alone. We think an AI tutor is the answer. We will know by March whether it works."

Now you promised an outcome, and you are allowed to change the solution when you learn something.

The three-part roadmap — use this structure:

PartConfidenceHow to talk about it
Now (this quarter)High — we are building itYou can commit to this
Next (next quarter)Medium — probably, if what we learn now says yes"Likely, not promised"
Later (the year)Low — the direction"This is where we're heading"
Always publish the "not doing" list. Writing "we are not building X this year, because Y" is worth more than half the roadmap. It stops the same conversation happening every month, and it tells your team what to refuse without asking you.
Say this

"I build the roadmap around problems and outcomes, not features and dates."

"I use three levels: now, next, later. Now is committed. Next is likely. Later is direction. Mixing them together is how an experiment becomes a promise by accident."

"And I publish what we are not doing, with the reason. That saves more time than the roadmap itself."

If they push: "But my CEO wants dates." → "That's fair, and some dates are real — a school year starts when it starts. I give firm dates for the current quarter, and ranges beyond that. What I won't do is give a firm date for something we haven't proven is possible yet. I'd rather say 'I'll know in three weeks' than give a date I'll have to break."

4Part 4 — Writing it down

Lesson 10The PRD — what it is and a template you can copy

PRD = Product Requirements Document. It is the document that says what we are building and why, so everyone builds the same thing.

Think of it like…

A brief for a builder. Not a drawing of every brick — that is the engineer's job. But clear enough that they do not build a garage when you wanted a kitchen.

Copy this template:

# [Feature name]

## 1. The problem
Who has this problem, and what happens to them today.
Evidence: what data or interviews tell us this is real.

## 2. Why now
Why this, before the other things we could do.

## 3. Who it is for
Which user. If it serves everyone, it probably serves nobody.

## 4. What success looks like
The number we expect to move, and by how much.
"We will know this worked if ___ goes from ___ to ___."

## 5. What we are building
Plain description. The user journey, step by step.

## 6. What we are NOT building
The most important section. Write it early.

## 7. Open questions
What we do not know yet, and who will answer it.

## 8. Risks
What could go wrong. For AI: what happens when it is wrong.
Section 6 is the one to mention in the interview. "What we are NOT building" prevents scope growing quietly. Without it, every conversation adds one more small thing, and three months later the feature is twice the size and nobody decided that.

Section 4 is the one juniors forget. If you cannot say which number should move, you do not understand the feature yet. Write it before you write section 5.
Say this

"My spec has eight parts, but three matter most. The problem with evidence. What success looks like — the actual number we expect to move. And what we are not building."

"That last one stops scope growing quietly. Without it, every meeting adds one small thing, and three months later the feature is twice the size and nobody decided that."

"I also write the success number before the solution. If I can't say which number should move, I don't understand the problem well enough yet."

Lesson 11User stories and acceptance criteria

A user story is one small piece of work, written from the user's side. Acceptance criteria say exactly when it is finished.

The format:

As a [who]
I want to [do what]
So that [why — the benefit]

Real example for your product:

As a grade 8 student
I want to ask the tutor about a question I got wrong
So that I understand my mistake instead of just seeing the right answer

ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA
✓ The tutor can see which question I got wrong and what I answered
✓ The tutor asks me what I tried before it explains anything
✓ The tutor never gives the final answer directly
✓ The answer shows which lesson it came from
✓ If the topic is not in my curriculum, the tutor says so
✓ The first words appear in under 3 seconds
✓ It works in Arabic and in French
Why acceptance criteria matter: without them, "done" is an opinion. The engineer thinks it is finished. You think it is not. You argue. With them, it is a checklist and there is nothing to argue about.

Notice criteria 3 and 5. Those are product rules, not technical details. That is your job to specify. An engineer will not invent "never give the final answer" on their own.
Say this

"A user story is one small piece of work written from the user's side: as a student, I want to, so that."

"Then acceptance criteria — the checklist that says when it is finished. Without them, 'done' is an opinion and you argue about it."

"And I put the product rules in the criteria. For a tutor, 'never gives the final answer directly' is a line I write. The engineer will not invent that rule for me."

5Part 5 — Working with the team

Lesson 12Agile and Scrum — what actually happens

Agile means: build a small piece, show it, learn, adjust. Scrum is one common way to do that, in two-week blocks called sprints.

Think of it like…

Old way: plan everything for a year, build for a year, show it at the end. If you were wrong, you wasted a year.

Agile: plan two weeks, build, show it, ask "is this right?", adjust. If you were wrong, you wasted two weeks.

The four meetings, and what you do in each:

MeetingWhenYour job in it
Sprint planningStart of the sprintExplain what matters most and why. The team decides how much they can take. You do not decide that.
Daily standupEvery day, 15 minListen. Catch blockers. Do not turn it into a status report for you.
Sprint review / demoEnd of the sprintSee the real thing working. Give feedback. Invite stakeholders.
RetrospectiveEnd of the sprintThe team talks about how they worked. Often you mostly listen — and you accept criticism of yourself.

The words you need:

BacklogThe list of everything we might build, in priority order. You own the order.
SprintA fixed block of time, usually 2 weeks.
Story pointsHow big a task is — relative, not in hours. The team estimates, not you.
VelocityHow many points the team finishes per sprint. Used to predict, never to push.
BlockerSomething stopping work. Your top priority to remove.
Definition of doneThe team's shared rule for when work is really finished (tested, reviewed, documented).
The mistake that makes engineers hate a PM: using velocity as pressure. "Last sprint you did 30 points, so this sprint do 35." Velocity is a prediction tool, not a target. Teams respond by inflating their estimates, and then the number means nothing.
Say this

"Agile means build a small piece, show it, learn, adjust. Scrum is one way to do that, in two-week sprints."

"In planning I explain what matters most and why. The team decides how much fits — that is their call, not mine."

"And I never use velocity as a target. It is for predicting, not for pushing. If you push with it, the team just makes the numbers bigger and the number stops meaning anything."

Lesson 13What a Product Manager does NOT do

Knowing the limits of the job is a sign of experience. Many candidates describe themselves doing everyone else's job.

You do NOT…Because…
Decide how to build itThat is the engineers' expertise. You give the problem and the constraints.
Decide how long it takesThe team estimates. You can ask "what would make this smaller?" — you cannot set the number.
Design the screensThat is the designer. You give the problem and the user, not the layout.
Manage the team's daily tasksThat is the team, or a scrum master. You manage the what, not the who does what today.
Write the tickets and disappearThat is a ticket writer, not a PM. You own the outcome.
The one exception worth saying: in a small team, you do a bit of everything. The difference is that you know which hat you are wearing. Say this — it shows you understand the theory and the reality.
Say this

"I decide what and why. I do not decide how — that is the engineers' expertise, and if I take that from them I lose the most valuable thing they have."

"I also do not set estimates. I can ask what would make it smaller. I cannot set the number."

"In a small team everyone does a bit of everything. The important thing is knowing which hat I am wearing at that moment."

Lesson 14Working with engineers — how to be trusted

Bring the problem, not the solution. That is the whole rule.

Think of it like…

Bad: "Build me a search box with a dropdown filter."

Good: "Students can't find last week's lesson. They give up after about 30 seconds. How could we fix that?"

In the second version, the engineer might say: "We don't need search. If we just put 'continue where you left off' on the home page, 80% of that problem disappears." That is a week of work saved, and you would never have thought of it.

Four things that build trust fast:

  • Answer questions quickly. A blocked engineer costs more than a wrong answer. Decide, and say you will change it if you were wrong.
  • Protect them from interruptions. When sales walks in with an urgent request, it comes to you, not to them.
  • Explain why, always. "Build this" gets you compliance. "Build this because students drop off here" gets you their brain.
  • Take the blame publicly. If the feature failed because it was the wrong idea, that is yours. Say it out loud.
Your advantage: you have been the engineer. You know what a vague spec feels like, and you know when "that's hard" is real. Say this — it is genuinely rare and it answers the "can you work with an ML team" question at the same time.
Say this

"I bring the problem, not the solution. If I say 'build me a search box', I get a search box. If I say 'students can't find last week's lesson and give up after 30 seconds', I often get a better and cheaper answer that I would never have thought of."

"I answer their questions fast. A blocked engineer is more expensive than a decision I might have to change later."

"And I have been on that side. I have been the developer receiving a vague spec. So I know what is missing before I send it."

6Part 6 — Stakeholders and politics

Lesson 15Who your stakeholders are and what each one wants

A stakeholder is anyone who cares about your product and is not on your team. Each one wants something different. Speak to each in their own language.

WhoWhat they actually wantSay it in their words
CEO / bossGrowth, money, no surprises"This affects retention and it is worth about X."
SalesThings that close deals"This unblocks the three schools you mentioned."
MarketingSomething to announce, and dates"Here is what is confirmed and what is not yet."
SupportFewer angry tickets"This removes the top complaint this quarter."
Content / teachersQuality and respect for their expertise"You decide the pedagogy. I'll bring you the data."
LegalNo risk, especially with children"Here is what data we collect and why."
The most useful habit

Translate your finding into their currency before you walk into the room.

You learned this at Jumia. A returns problem described as a rate went nowhere. The same problem described as a cost got fixed within a week. Same fact. Different unit. Different result.

Say this

"I translate the same finding into each person's language before the meeting."

"I learned this the hard way at Jumia. I presented a returns problem as a rate and nothing happened. I presented the same problem as a cost and it was fixed in a week. Same fact, different unit."

Lesson 16When a senior person wants something you disagree with

This happens constantly. How you handle it decides how much people trust you.

The five steps:

1. Find the real needThey said "add a leaderboard". Ask why. Maybe the real problem is "students stop after two weeks". A leaderboard is their solution, not their problem.
2. Agree on the problem"So we want students to come back more. Yes?" Now you are on the same side.
3. Bring evidence, not opinion"Here is what our data shows about why students stop."
4. Offer a cheaper test"Instead of building it for everyone, can we test it with 200 students for two weeks?"
5. If they still say do it — do it wellDisagree and commit. Then measure it honestly.
Step 5 matters most. Sometimes you lose. If you then build it badly, or quietly, or with an "I told you so" face — you have damaged the relationship more than the feature ever could. Build it properly, measure it honestly, and let the number speak. If you were right, everyone sees it without you saying anything.
Say this

"First I find the real need. If someone asks for a leaderboard, the leaderboard is their solution. The problem might be that students stop after two weeks."

"Then I bring evidence instead of my opinion, and I try to offer a cheaper way to find out — test it with 200 students instead of building it for everyone."

"And if they still want it, I build it properly and measure it honestly. Disagree and commit. Doing it badly to prove I was right is the worst thing I could do."

Lesson 17Status updates — the template

Send the same short update every week. Boring and regular beats detailed and occasional.

SUBJECT: [Product] — week of 12 October

🟢 ON TRACK   AI tutor pilot — grade 8 maths
   Shipped to 200 students this week. First numbers on Friday.

🟡 AT RISK    Content tagging
   We are 3 weeks behind. Reason: 2 experts moved to exam season.
   What I need: 1 more reviewer, or we move the date to 15 December.
   Decision needed by: Friday.

🔴 BLOCKED    Teacher dashboard
   Waiting on the content map. Restarts when tagging is done.

DECISIONS MADE
   - We are not launching in Gulf dialect this quarter. Quality is not there.

NEXT WEEK
   - First learning results from the pilot
   - Decision on the tagging resource
The two rules:
  • Bad news early and plainly. A problem told in week 2 is a problem. The same problem discovered in week 8 is a crisis, and it is now your credibility as well.
  • Never say "at risk" without saying what you need. Always attach the decision you want and the deadline for it. Otherwise you have just transferred anxiety with no way to act on it.
Say this

"I send the same short update every week. Green, yellow, red. What we decided. What is next."

"Two rules. Bad news goes out early — a problem in week 2 is a problem, the same problem found in week 8 is a crisis. And I never say 'at risk' without saying what I need and by when."

7Part 7 — Launch and after

Lesson 18How to launch

Launch slowly on purpose. Especially with AI, and especially with children.

1  INTERNAL      The team uses it. You use it every day for a week.
2  SMALL PILOT   100–500 users you can actually talk to.
                 Read what happens. Not dashboards — real sessions.
3  PERCENTAGE    5% → 25% → 50% → 100%, watching the numbers each time
4  FULL          Everyone

AT EVERY STEP YOU NEED:
   ✓ a way to turn it off instantly (without a new release)
   ✓ a clear rule for when to stop and go back
   ✓ someone watching the numbers, by name
   ✓ support and teachers told in advance
The kill switch is not optional for an AI feature. If something goes wrong with a tutor talking to children, you need it off in one minute, not in one release cycle. Say this — it shows you have thought about the worst day, not just the launch day.
Say this

"I launch in stages. Internal, then a small pilot I can actually talk to, then a percentage rollout."

"For an AI feature with children, two things are mandatory. A switch to turn it off instantly without a release. And real people reading real conversations in the first weeks — not just dashboards. That is where you find the problems no test predicted."

Lesson 19After launch — and how to kill a feature

Most teams launch and move on. Checking afterwards, and removing what failed, is what separates a senior PM.

Two weeks after launch, answer four questions:

1Did the number we promised actually move?
2Did anything else get worse? (the guardrails)
3Are people using it the way we expected — or differently?
4What did we learn that changes the next thing we build?

Killing a feature — the rules:

  • Decide the rule before you launch. "If fewer than 5% use it after 8 weeks, we remove it." Decide it while nobody is emotionally attached.
  • Removing costs something too. Every feature you keep must be maintained, tested, explained and supported forever. A feature nobody uses is not free.
  • Tell the truth about why. "We were wrong about the problem" is a fine reason and it teaches the team something. "It didn't get priority" teaches nothing.
Why this is hard: people become attached to what they built. You will be attached too. That is exactly why the rule must exist before launch, in writing.
Say this

"Two weeks after launch I check four things: did the number move, did anything else get worse, are people using it the way we expected, and what did we learn."

"And I decide the kill rule before launch — while nobody is attached yet. 'If fewer than 5% use it after eight weeks, we remove it.'"

"Because a feature nobody uses is not free. We maintain it, test it and explain it forever."

8Part 8 — Managing other PMs

Lesson 20What changes when you lead PMs The job asks for this

You stop making the decisions. You start making sure good decisions get made.

Think of it like…

You were the best picker in the warehouse. Now you manage the pickers.

If you keep picking, you are doing one person's job and nobody is managing. If you coach, you get the work of the whole team — but you have to let them be slower than you at first.

The four things a PM lead does:

1. Set the directionThey own their area. You own how the areas fit together.
2. Coach the thinkingAsk questions instead of giving answers. "How do you know that's the problem?" "What would prove you wrong?"
3. Hold the seamsThe gaps between two PMs' areas are where products break. Nobody owns them unless you do.
4. Remove the blockersGet them access, budget, and the difficult meeting they cannot get themselves.
The failure mode to name out loud: taking the decision away from them because you would be faster. It feels efficient. It teaches them nothing, and next time they wait for you instead of deciding. Saying this proves you understand the job even without having done it.
Say this — the honest version

"I have not managed product managers. I have led a team — a warehouse operation across shifts, with performance management and the hard conversations. So leading people is not new."

"What I think managing PMs needs is coaching judgement, not reviewing output. Asking 'how do you know that's the real problem' instead of answering it for them."

"And holding the seams. The gaps between two PMs' areas are where products break, and nobody owns them unless the lead does."

"The failure mode I would watch for in myself is taking the decision because I would be faster. It feels efficient and it teaches them nothing."

9Part 9 — Interview questions on the PM craft

These are the questions the AI file does not cover. All answers use your real experience.

1. What does a product manager do?
Say this

"Five things. Find the real problem, decide what we do first, explain it clearly, help the team ship it, and prove whether it worked."

"The part people underestimate is that a PM has no authority. I cannot order the engineers or the designer. So the whole job is having a clear problem, real evidence, and being able to explain it. People follow the reasoning, not the title."

2. How do you decide what to build next?
Say this

"I start by checking that the ideas are even solving the same goal. Usually two or three are the same need described by different people, and one or two don't fit the strategy at all. Removing those is cheaper than ranking them."

"Then for each one: how big is the problem, how sure am I that this solves it, how much work is it, and does it fit where we are going."

"I use RICE when a decision is contested. But I'd be honest that RICE isn't science — I choose the numbers. Its real value is that it forces us to say why we think the impact is high. The argument becomes about evidence instead of about who is most senior."

"And I always argue separately for two things that score badly in any framework: the boring foundations that make later work cheap, and short experiments that buy us information."

3. Tell me about a time you said no to an important stakeholder. STAR
Say this

Situation. "At Jumia, returns were going up. The proposal on the table was to inspect every outbound order — permanent extra cost on every single order. It had support, because it was the obvious answer."

Task. "I wasn't convinced, because our existing packing checks weren't finding much. But 'I'm not convinced' is an opinion and it loses to a confident proposal."

Action. "So I got evidence. The data didn't exist — returns were logged as free text, which can't be analysed. So I changed the collection first: a fixed list of reasons, short enough that people wouldn't just choose 'other'."

"A few months later I had real data. The dominant cause wasn't damage — it was products not matching their listings, and it was concentrated in a small number of sellers."

"I didn't present it as 'your idea is wrong'. I showed what the money would buy in each case: inspection would add cost to 100% of orders to fix a minority cause. Fixing the listings was a fraction of the cost and addressed the real driver."

Result. "The inspection plan was dropped. We fixed the listings and put the worst sellers on corrective plans. Returns came down at a fraction of the cost."

"What I take from it: the most valuable thing I did was fix how we collected information, before I could answer anything at all."

Why this works as a PM story: it is prioritisation, evidence, stakeholder management and saying no — all in one. And the ending, "first I had to fix the instrumentation", is exactly what product work really looks like.
4. How do you work with engineers?
Say this

"I bring the problem and the constraints, not the solution. If I say 'build me a search box', I get a search box. If I say 'students can't find last week's lesson and give up after 30 seconds', I often get a simpler answer I would never have thought of."

"I answer their questions fast. A blocked engineer costs more than a decision I might have to change later."

"And I have been on the other side. I spent two and a half years as a developer, so I know what a vague spec feels like, and I know when 'that's hard' is real and when it means 'I don't want to'."

5. A feature you launched didn't work. What do you do?
Say this

"First I check that it really didn't work, and that we aren't measuring it too early. Learning effects take weeks, so a flat number after five days means nothing."

"Then I want to know which part failed. Did people not find it? That's discovery or design. Did they find it and not use it? Then the problem wasn't real, or our solution didn't fit. Did they use it and nothing improved? Then the solution doesn't solve the problem. Three very different lessons."

"Then I decide: fix it, or remove it. And I try to have decided that rule before launch, while nobody was attached to it yet."

"I'd also say it out loud to the team and to my manager. If it was the wrong idea, that was my call, not theirs. Taking that publicly is part of the job."

6. How do you handle a stakeholder who keeps changing their mind?
Say this

"Usually it means we never agreed on the problem — only on a solution. Solutions change easily. Problems don't."

"So I go back one level: 'can we agree on what we're trying to fix, and how we'll know it worked?' Once that's written down, new ideas get measured against it instead of replacing it."

"I also write decisions down and share them — short, in the weekly update. 'We decided X on this date, for this reason.' Most changes of mind are actually people who were never told the decision, or forgot the reason."

"And if it keeps happening, I'd say it directly and kindly: every change costs us two weeks, so can we agree to hold this until the end of the quarter."

7. Two teams want opposite things. How do you decide?
Say this

"I get both in one room rather than negotiating separately by email. Most of these fights survive because each side only hears my version of the other."

"Then I make them argue against the goal, not against each other. Not 'sales wants X, content wants Y' but 'which of these moves the number we all agreed on'."

"Often there's a smaller version that gives one side 80% of what they need, and I look for that before I pick a winner."

"If there's genuinely no overlap, I decide, I say why in writing, and I tell the side that lost directly — not by letting them read it in a document. And I say what would change it."

8. How do you measure success?
Say this

"Before we build, not after. If I can't say which number should move and by roughly how much, I don't understand the feature well enough yet."

"I use three layers. One main number that says the product is working. A few supporting numbers that explain why it moved. And guardrails — numbers that must not get worse."

"For a learning product I'd be careful about engagement. More time on the platform can mean the student is lost, not engaged. Good teaching means less time for the same result. So the number I'd watch is whether the student can do the next problem without help."

9. What's the hardest part of being a PM?
Say this

"Saying no, and doing it in a way that keeps the relationship."

"The decision itself is usually not that hard once you have the evidence. The hard part is that the person asking has a real problem, and you are telling them it will stay unsolved for another quarter. If you do that badly, they stop bringing you information — and then you're deciding blind."

"The other hard part is that you're responsible for the outcome and you control almost nothing. You can't order anyone. You only have the quality of your reasoning."

10. What would you do in your first 90 days? Common closer
Say this

"First month — learn, and hold my opinions. Talk to students and teachers directly, and watch real sessions, including ones that go badly. Understand what's already shipped and what the numbers really are. Meet the engineers and the ML team early, because whether they trust me decides whether I get told the truth later."

"I'd also check two things specifically: what we measure and whether anyone believes it, and what our test set for the AI looks like. In my experience the first real finding is always that two teams have different numbers for the same thing."

"Second month — fix the measurement and agree the priorities. Write down the metric framework and get agreement, because arguments about priorities are usually arguments about definitions."

"Third month — ship something and commit to a plan. One real improvement delivered, so the team sees what working with me is like. Then a roadmap for two quarters with clear bets and clear non-goals."

"What I would not do is arrive in week two with a big strategy. I'd rather earn that with one good decision first."

10Part 10 — Words and templates

Product management words, in one line each

WordSimple meaning
DiscoveryThe work of finding out what to build, before building it.
DeliveryThe work of actually building and shipping it.
BacklogThe ordered list of everything we might build.
RoadmapThe plan of which problems we solve, and roughly when.
PRD / specThe document that says what we're building and why.
User storyOne small piece of work written from the user's side.
Acceptance criteriaThe checklist that says when it's really finished.
MVPThe smallest version that lets us learn if this works.
Scope creepThe feature quietly growing bigger with nobody deciding it.
SprintA fixed block of work time, usually two weeks.
VelocityHow much the team finishes per sprint. For predicting, not pushing.
BlockerSomething stopping work. Your top priority to remove.
StakeholderAnyone who cares about your product and isn't on your team.
North star metricThe one number that best shows the product is working.
Guardrail metricA number that must not get worse while you improve another.
Leading indicatorAn early number that predicts the slow one. (Sign-ups predict revenue.)
Lagging indicatorThe slow, true number. (Exam results.)
CohortA group of users who started at the same time, compared over time.
ChurnPeople who stop using or stop paying.
RetentionPeople who come back.
ActivationA new user reaching their first real moment of value.
Feature flagA switch to turn a feature on or off without a new release.
DogfoodingUsing your own product every day.
Disagree and commitYou lost the argument. You support the decision fully anyway.
Technical debtShortcuts taken earlier that make everything slower now.

Template 1 — PRD (copy this)

# [Feature name]

## The problem        Who suffers, what happens today, evidence it's real
## Why now            Why this before the alternatives
## Who it's for       One user type. Not "everyone".
## Success            "We'll know this worked if ___ goes from ___ to ___"
## What we're building  The user journey, step by step
## What we're NOT building   ← write this early
## Open questions     What we don't know, and who answers it
## Risks              What could go wrong. For AI: what if it's wrong?

🟢 ON TRACK   [what] — [one line of progress]

🔴 BLOCKED    [what] — [waiting on what]

DECISIONS MADE   [decision + one-line reason]

Template 3 — user interview (copy this)

1  "Tell me about the last time you [studied for an exam]."
2  "Walk me through what you actually did. Step by step."
3  "What was the most frustrating part?"
4  "Why was that frustrating?"           ← ask why 3 times
5  "What did you do about it?"
6  "What have you tried before? Why did you stop?"
7  "If that problem disappeared tomorrow, what would change for you?"

NEVER:  "Would you use…"  ·  "Do you like…"  ·  "How often would you…"
ALWAYS: be quiet for 3 seconds after they answer.
